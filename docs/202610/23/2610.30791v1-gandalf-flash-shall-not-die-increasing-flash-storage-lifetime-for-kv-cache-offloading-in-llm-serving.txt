## Abstract 

KV caching reduces the serving cost of large language models (LLMs), but the growing KV cache footprint is increasingly ex-ceeds GPU and host-memory capacity. Therefore, people use SSD to expand the KV cache storage capacity with high I/O bandwidth. However, in production LLM workload, such as multi-round con-versation and LLM agents, the total amount of KV cache writes significantly exceeds SSD capacity, shortening SSD lifetime from years to months. To improve SSD lifetime, a natural solution is to reduce the KV cache writes by gating the KV cache writes via his-torical access patterns. However, that significantly lowers the KV cache hit rate (and increase the LLM serving cost) since KV caches from different attention types might have identical historical access pattern but different future access patterns. Our insight is that, to reduce KV cache writes without hurting KV cache hit rate, one need to not only consider the access pattern, but also application-level information such as the attention type of KV cache. Leveraging this observation, we propose Gandalf , the first SSD KV cache offload-ing system with SSD life time awareness. Gandalf prioritize the admission of full-attention KV cache into SSD over the cache gener-ated by other types of attention, which significantly increases SSD lifetime without sacrificing cache hit rate. Our evaluation shows that Gandalf extends the SSD lifetime by 7.3 × on average, while having negligible impact on cache hit rate across multiple workload traces and wide range of hardware capacity. Gandalf design can be extended to other flash-based storage, such as High Bandwidth Flash (HBF). 

## 1 Introduction 

Large language models (LLMs) have demonstrated strong capabili-ties in multi-round conversations and agentic workflows, garnering wide adoption. These workloads accumulate conversation histories, tool outputs, and intermediate states, resulting in increasingly long and frequently reused contexts. However, recomputing the atten-tion key-value (KV) states for these contexts at every rounds sub-stantially increases inference latency and cost. Hence, people store all KV caches in their system to avoid recomputation and increase cache hit rate [1, 32]. Production workloads generates Terabytes of KV-cache per day at the amount that exceeds the capacity of GPU and host memory, motivating the use of higher-capacity storage tiers. Flash-based storage, e.g. NVMe SSDs, provides a scalable and comparatively cost-effective tier for storing these caches [29].

This additional capacity comes with an endurance challenge because SSDs can only handle a finite volume of writes before they are worn out [4]. Under production workloads, aggressive KV-cache admission improves cache-hit rate but generates substantial write volume, exhausting SSD endurance within days to months. Therefore, existing production-level systems came up with simple filtering techniques that gates SSD admission, trading potential cache hits for fewer writes. Frequency-based solutions, for example, offloads a KV cache to SSD only if it has demonstrated 𝑁 × reuse, with 𝑁 as a hyperparameter [30, 35]. However, even with such filtering, the aggregate KV-cache write volume remains excessive for practical SSD endurance. This raises a central question: 

How can we improve the SSD lifetime without sacrificing KV caching performance benefit? 

The key challenge lies in predicting the future reuse of KV caches such that we can selectively retain only the most reusable caches to make the most of SSD’s capacity. Future prediction is difficult, but we found application-specific property that strongly correlates with reuse pattern. We leverage two observations: (1) Recent LLMs in-creasingly employ hybrid architectures that combine full-attention layers with linear- or sparse-attention layers, and (2) These dif-ferent attention layer types produce KV caches with substantially different reuse patterns under emerging mainstream workloads: multi-round conversations and agentic workflows. states from linear- and sparse-attention layers are reused less fre-quently than KV caches from full-attention layers. Consider a multi-round conversation workload use-case, which reuse pattern illustrated in Table 1 . At the beginning of each new round, the full-attention KV entries for the entire preceding con-text is needed. This pattern repeats for every new round, hence these KV entries are reused across all future rounds. In contrast, a linear-attention layer’s state and sliding-window cache is only needed to generate the immediate next round’s response, and is then discarded, hence every state is only reused once. This reuse pattern heterogeneity motivates attention-type-aware cache placement. Writing large, one-time use linear-attention states to SSD provides little cache hit benefit while consuming both write endurance and cache capacity. Layer type can therefore serve as a practical placement signal, allowing us to utilize SSDs to ex-pand cache capacity while minimizing endurance loss. We propose an attention layer-aware placement policy that caches short-lived linear-attention states in host memory while keeping longer-lived full-attention KV caches on SSD. However, there are 2 practical challenges that entail this place-ment policy. 

First , this placement policy is too rigid: the size of CPU DRAM and SSD is fixed, but workload can generate different amounts of different attention states. Particularly, a workload with many concurrent sessions can generate a large number of reusable linear-attention states that exceeds the available DRAM capacity, simi-larly, a workload with long contexts can generate a large number of reusable full-attention KV caches that exceeds the available SSD capacity. We call this a hardware-to-utilization imbalance. This imbalance forces frequent cache eviction on the saturated tier de-spite available capacity elsewhere in the storage hierarchy, thus lowering cache-hit rate. Worse, if the overutilization happens in SSD, the resulting write churn can consume its endurance budget more rapidly and further shorten its lifetime. Therefore, we design a capacity-aware placement policy that filters SSD admission based on utilization and pressure of each tier, balancing cache hit rate against SSD endurance. 

Second , such rigid placement moves cache hit from DRAM to SSD, which can hurt TTFT because SSD bandwidth is substantially lower than DRAM bandwidth. Hence, transferring large full-attention KV caches from SSD may hurt TTFT. We explore the potential of attention-type-aware placement us-ing an offline oracle that jointly optimizes SSD admission and cache eviction decisions, biased towards our tier placement. The oracle reveals two complementary design principles. First , placement must adapt to tier capacity and selectively persist KV caches on SSD to preserve the prefix-hit boundary. Second , admission and eviction must be optimized jointly: admitting a new cache has cost and ben-efit. It benefits future reuse, but it has write cost and replacement cost—cost of replacing other cache entries, may or may not already pay for its promised benefit. An SSD admission is beneficial only when its expected reuse value exceeds the sum of write cost and the value of the entries it evicts. Together, these principles charac-terize the best attainable trade-off among SSD lifetime, cache-hit rate, and TTFT. We then distill the oracle’s decisions into an online, capacity-aware policy that estimates future reuse from historical access patterns.

Our work makes the following contributions: (1) We are the first to show that the SSD lifetime is significantly lower than its factory guarantee in production-scale SSD-based KV cache offloading. ## 2 Motivation 2.1 KV Caches Exceed Memory Capacity 

Over the past years, the increasing intelligence and context window size of LLMs empowered multi-round conversations and agentic workflows. These types of workloads continuously accumulate conversation history, intermediate reasoning, tool outputs, and subagent results. Unlike single-turn requests, these workloads re-peatedly extend and reuse an existing prefix, causing each session’s KV-cache footprint to grow throughout its lifetime. Hence, the aggregate KV cache sizes saved across requests and sessions are getting enormous. Long contexts also make cache misses increasingly expensive. When a reusable prefix is not cached, the serving system must recompute its KV states by prefilling all preceding tokens before generating a response. In workloads with long context sizes, such as multi-round conversation, agentic workflow, and long document processing, this repeatedly consumes scarce GPU computation, increasing TTFT and serving cost. People therefore seek to retain as much reusable KV state as possible, effectively trading storage capacity for computation. Our analysis of two one-day SemiAnalysis Claude Code traces [40] estimates that representative hybrid models generate 81–240 TB of KV-cache state per day ( Table 2 ). At peak, the concurrently active KV-cache working set reaches 8.6TB, exceeding practical host-memory capacity, hence provisioning sufficient CPU DRAM would be prohibitively expensive. ## 2.3 KV Caching is Rapidly Killing SSDs 

Under frontier model and agentic workloads, our analysis shows that even a 30-TB enterprise SSD can exhaust its rated TBW in less than one year. Smaller enterprise SSDs wear out even faster, with a 2-TB drive reaching its rated TBW in just days. In §2.2, we discussed that SSD wear is a product of host-write volume and device-level write amplification. We target host writes by reducing the KV-cache writes issued to SSD. Tiered KV caches commonly use write-through or write-back offloading. 

Write-through immediately copies every admitted KV states to SSD, maximizing availability at multiple tiers, but generating sub-stantial SSD write. This duplication preserves the entry after DRAM eviction, but wastes SSD endurance when all of its reuse occurs in a short time while it remains in DRAM. The SSD copy then consumes write budget without serving a cache hit. 

Write-back persists demoted KV states from higher storage tier regardless of its expected reuse. This policy still writes every DRAM eviction to SSD, including states that contribute little to future cache hits. 

Selective write-through reduces this traffic using a fixed reuse threshold; for example, NVIDIA Dynamo offloads a block only after its access frequency reaches two [30]. However, frequency-only filtering ignores state size, layer-specific reuse, tier occupancy, and the remaining write budget, allowing large, short-lived states to consume substantial endurance for little benefit. 

## 2.4 How does prior work address SSD endurance? 

SSD endurance has long been a concern in flash-based caching systems. Traditional systems, such as Flashcache [11] and Google’s Colossus Flash Cache [51], use SSDs to accelerate access to persis-tent data. Recent studies show that KV-cache offloading introduces similar endurance challenges in LLM serving [23, 42]. Existing approaches can be grouped into three categories. 

Increasing physical write endurance. Hardware provisioning dis-tributes writes across more SSDs, aggregating write budget and reducing the per-device write rate, thereby increasing aggregate write endurance at the cost of additional acquisition, power, and maintenance costs [19]. ## 2.6 Key Observation: Hybrid model has heterogeneous reuse pattern 

Dense models use full attention across all layers. When a cached context is resumed, the KV entries of every tokens in the sequence prefix are reused. Thus, storing a larger portion of the prefix allows more recomputation to be avoided, creating a near one-for-one trade-off between SSD writes and cache-hit rate. Each layer and token’s admitted KV entry provides comparable reuse value, and reducing SSD writes generally sacrifices a corresponding caching opportunity. This creates the apparent one-for-one trade-off be-tween SSD lifetime and cache-hit rate. Hybrid models break this trade-off due to its model architec-ture: combining full-attention layers with sparse attention layers, such as linear and sliding-window attention. As shown in Table 1 ,cached states from different layer types exhibit substantially non-uniform reuse patterns, where linear state is reused much less than full attention KV cache. We observed that under append-only workloads such as multi-round conversation, all prefix tokens’ full-attention KV caches are reused in every subsequent round, so their reuse count increases as the conversation grows. By contrast, each sparse-attention state is typically used only once—to generate the immediately following round—before being superseded by a newer state. Uniformly writing these short-lived states to SSD is inefficient. Write-through immediately persists them, while write-back may persist them upon eviction, even though they are likely to be super-seded after only one reuse. When the latest linear-attention state can remain in DRAM until the next round, its SSD copy provides no additional cache hit but still consumes SSD endurance and capacity. Such writes may also evict more reusable full-attention KV entries, further reducing cache effectiveness.

## 3.1 Attention type-aware placement 

Hybrid models produce cached states with substantially different sizes and reuse patterns. Full-attention layers retain per-token KV entries, so their cache grows with the prefix length, whereas linear-attention layers maintain a fixed-size recurrent state at each check-point. Sliding-window layers retain only the most recent 𝑊 tokens and can therefore use the same placement policy as linear-attention layers. The state types also differ substantially in size. Across our evaluated models, a single linear-attention state occupies as much space as the full-attention KV entries for 2 𝑘 –16 𝑘 tokens. However, reuse is only meaningful when all types of states are available at the same prefix boundary. This heterogeneity in size and reuse pattern brings 2 opportu-nities: (1) not all KV state needs to be cached, and (2) where an admitted state should be placed. The heterogeneity creates two distinct cache-management decisions. First, admission determines which generated states provide sufficient reuse value to retain. Sec-ond, placement determines the storage tier in which each admitted state should reside. Prior works has solved “which KV state should be cached” by probing the reuse boundary [33, 46]. These mechanisms address which states should be retained, but they do not determine whether an admitted state should reside in DRAM or in SSD. Our work is aimed to improve SSD lifetime by managing KV cache placement leveraging its attention layer type to give a hint on the most suitable placement in the storage tier. We therefore retain linear state in DRAM and full attention KV caches in SSD. Each linear state generated at the end of each conversation round is put in DRAM while the full attention KV cache is put on SSD. When the storage tier (DRAM/SSD) is out of space, the least-recently-used cache on the tier is evicted.

𝐻 left sums the remaining hits of the evicted candidates. Otherwise it keeps the SSD as it is and drops the new object.

## 3.4 Optimization 

Scoring every cached chunk for every eviction is expensive: the cache holds millions of chunks, and their probability-weighted scores evolve over time. Gandalf avoids this cost with three obser-vations. 

First , only leaves can be dropped, and only root-side DRAM nodes can be moved to SSD. Gandalf only maintains these two sets in an incremental manner. Dropping a leaf exposes at most its parent as a new leaf, while moving a root-side node exposes its children as new move candidates. Thus, recomputing the scores of the caches only happens when there is cache pressure, to a small number of items. 

Second , scores in Equation (7) change as objects age, but remain fixed within a request. Gandalf keeps each tier’s leaf candidates in a min-heap ordered by their probability-weighted drop scores 𝐻 𝑑 .A heap is rebuilt only when first queried in a new request and is reused for all subsequent evictions in that request. New leaves are inserted immediately, while stale entries are discarded lazily from the heap top. 

Third , Gandalf partitions root-side DRAM candidates by cache type: linear and full. Within each size class, the candidate with the longest estimated remaining life has the lowest SSD write fee. A lazy heap ordered by remaining life therefore identifies this candidate without rescoring the class again during the request. By default, Gandalf compares only the longest-lived candidate from each size class. In later evaluation, we show that comparing only one candidate does not sacrifice performance. A separate lazy min-heap over SSD leaves identifies the lowest-valued SSD victim, and hence the replacement cost when DRAM pressure triggers a move from DRAM to SSD such that an object needs to be evicted from SSD to make room. After the heaps are built, each decision examines only their heads, rather than rescoring or sorting all cache objects. 

## 4 Implementation 

Verifying the simulator. We first ran vLLM [18] and connect it with LMCache [7] on RawBlock mode, with a Claude Code trace, then attaching an eBPF-based logger to dump out the storage-facing trace. This includes columns such as: timestamp since first run, type of operation (R/W), logical block address (LBA) accessed, size, and number of pages. We then replayed this trace on FEMU [21] with a 1TB @ 0.3 DWPD for 5 years SSD configuration to observe SSD lifetime given a KV cache offloading workload, resulting to our finding that KV cache is killing SSD. From this result, we obtained the SSD lifetime duration. Then, we build a trace-based simulator that estimates SSD lifetime by the TBW, and confirm the lifetime using the SSD lifetime we obtained by using SSD emulator. Simula-tor are needed to test all of our experiments prior to implementing in real hardware, which will effectively kills SSD for sure. After all technique was confirmed, we move our technique to real pro-duction system, involving LMCache [7] deployed as a backend for vLLM [18] inference engine. 

SSD lifetime estimation. Specifically, given that we confirmed the SSD lifetime using an emulator in the small scale, we estimate SSD lifetime by the TBW (Total Bytes Written). Once one trace is replayed, we can obtain the total write volume to SSD. Assuming that this trace is replayed over and over in production system, we can then obtain the total write volume per day, and then estimate the SSD lifetime by dividing the TBW by the total write volume per day. 

Transfer between tiers. Transfer between one tier and an-other assumes modes two-hops, using CPU as a staging buffer. Hit served from SSD must be transferred to CPU DRAM adhering SSD read bandwidth and SSD transfer bandwidth; hit served from CPU DRAM will go to GPU memory adhering CPU-to-GPU PCIe bandwidth; 

Hardware specs. The simulator models the hardware specs of the system that affects the transfer between SSD, CPU DRAM, and GPU HBM. We assume a specific GPU model, check the tokens per second throughput, and every generated KV cache is directly saved in the KV cache storage, e.g. LMCache [7]. For the SSD model, the simulator supports specifying DWPD (Drive Writes Per Day) and capacity. However, for our evaluation, we assume 1 DWPD (Drive Writes Per Day) for 5 years, which is a majority specification for enterprise SSDs. We check the SSD read and transfer bandwidth via FIO [2]. We then vary the SSD capacity for the evaluation. 

Known limitation. The simulator assumes write-amplification factor (WAF) to be 1, since the simulator does not model the SSD internal operations, such as garbage collection, wear leveling, and other internal operations that may affect the SSD lifetime. Hence, the simulator may overestimate the SSD lifetime for all the evalu-ated techniques, Gandalf and baselines. However, since WAF is implicated by the fast write rate, if we reduce the write volume using Gandalf , we can still reduce the WAF and improve the SSD lifetime.

## 5.6 Where does the improvement come from? 

Avoid writing non-reused linear states in SSD. Gandalf avoids writing linear states that will not be reused multiple times, such as those at every turn of a multi-turn conversation, to the SSD. LRU-K at 𝐾 = 1 writes 120.6k linear states to the SSD, and only 21% of them are ever hit there: 43% are never requested again, and 45% had already served their one hit from DRAM before being moved to the SSD, i.e., the state of a turn that the next turn already reused. A larger 𝐾 does not fix this. LRU-K [35] counts references per object, which captures hotness for objects that persist across requests, such as a user’s KV cache in generative recommendation. A linear state, however, is superseded at every turn and is referenced at most about twice, so it reaches 𝐾 = 2 references only after its one reuse is over: LRU-K drops a state that leaves DRAM before the next turn arrives, and writes one that the next turn has already reused. Gandalf instead decides by whether a state will be reused later, not by how often it was referenced: it writes 8.8 × fewer linear states than LRU-K at 𝐾 = 1, and 57% of them are hit on the SSD. With 𝛼 = 0, Gandalf has the lowest miss ratio of all policies, 0.603, and its SSD still lasts 1.4 × longer than LRU’s (4.95 against 3.46 days), because what it writes is read more: its SSD serves 1.8 × as many full-attention hits (2.04M against 1.14M) and 2.2 × as many linear-state hits (38.9k against 18.0k). 

MeritKV. MeritKV admits an object to the SSD only when the prefill it saves outweighs the expected reuse latency plus the write cost, but it admits the first three demands of every object uncondi-tionally to learn its reuse. Most linear states are written before this estimate applies: 97% of the linear states it writes had never been hit before the write. It still writes 36.1TB (4.0 days), only 5.6% of its linear-state writes are hit on the SSD, and it misses 0.807 of chunks. 

HotPrefix. HotPrefix admits a prefix to DRAM only if it has been requested at least 𝐹 times while DRAM has room, and otherwise only if its frequency times its recency clock beats the coldest DRAM entry; a refused prefix is dropped rather than written to the SSD. This cuts its writes to 4.4TB, 9.6 × fewer than LRU, and its SSD lasts 33 days. The filter, however, does not tell reused linear states from the rest: 97% of the linear states it writes had never been hit before, 51% are never requested again, and only 17% are hit on the SSD. The dropped prefixes become misses, so its miss ratio is 0.802; Gandalf at 𝛼 = 1 lasts 7.6 × longer at a 0.016 higher miss ratio. ## 5.8 How much is the cache load latency affected? 

We approximate the cache load latency of a request as the maxi-mum of the time to transfer its cached KV to GPU HBM and the time to recompute its missed tokens, since the two overlap. The transfer uses DRAM as a staging buffer: KV on the SSD is first read into DRAM (10GB/s), then every cached byte is sent to GPU HBM (50GB/s); recomputation runs at 60k tokens/s. Figure 13 shows the average cache load latency against SSD lifetime on ClaudeCode with Kimi-K3, 100GB of DRAM and a 1TB SSD. The baselines with the lowest load latency, Marconi at 𝛼 = 0 and LRU, load in 0.66s and their SSDs last 4 months. Gandalf at 𝛼 = 1 lasts 4.2 × longer (17.0 months) at a load latency of 0.78s, 0.12s higher. It reads more of its hits from the SSD, which is slower than DRAM, and its miss ratio is 0.01 higher (0.056 against 0.045), so slightly more tokens are recomputed. At a similar load latency, Gandalf lasts 3.7 × longer than LRU-K at 𝐾 = 2 (21.2 against 5.7 months, 0.79s against 0.81s), and with 𝛼 = 64 it lasts 5.3 × longer than LRU-K at 𝐾 = 3 while still loading faster (38.6 against 7.3 months, 0.91s against 0.93s). However, more recomputation is not always worse. Every write wears out the SSD, effectively using a portion of its purchase price, so the cost of a request is the GPU cost of recomputing its missed tokens plus the SSD price share. Figure 14 shows this cost as an average per request, choosing the cheapest hyperparameters for each policy. SSD wear makes up 66–86% of the cost of LRU, Marconi and LRU-K, and the cheapest baseline, LRU-K at 𝐾 = 2, costs $0.74 per 1k requests. Gandalf at 𝛼 = 1 costs $0.30 per 1k requests, 2.5 × less: it recomputes more than LRU and Marconi ($0.13 against $0.11 per 1k requests), but its SSD wear is 3.0 × lower than that of any baseline ($0.16 against $0.49–0.73). ## 5.9 The optimization does not affect the performance negatively. 

To keep each decision cheap, the move door (§3.4) checks, for each slot size, only the longest-lived DRAM entry as a candidate to move to the SSD. A move is then blocked whenever that one entry’s score falls below its write fee, even if another entry of the same size would be worth writing. To measure what this costs, we let the move door check up to 64 candidates per slot size, with Kimi-K3, 100GB of DRAM, a 1TB SSD and 𝛼 = 1. Checking more candidates does not lower Gandalf ’s miss ratio: it stays at 0.823 on TraceA and 0.036 on AIPerf for every number of candidates. On TraceA, the extra candidates only move entries that are not read again, so the SSD writes grow by 1.4% (501 to 508GiB) and the SSD lifetime shortens from 271 to 268 days; on AIPerf, the first candidate is never blocked, so the result is identical. A single candidate per slot size therefore loses no hits. 

## 6 Related Work 

Tiered KV-cache systems. CachedAttention, FlashGen, LMCache, and Mooncake extend GPU memory with host DRAM, SSD, or distributed storage [7, 13, 16, 36], while TENURE and placement studies decide where KV caches should reside across these tiers [28, 43]. InfiniGen and Tutti instead reduce lower-tier access overhead through selective fetching and GPU-directed SSD I/O [20, 37]. These systems primarily optimize capacity, latency, or throughput rather than SSD endurance. 

KV admission, eviction, and compression. Prior policies predict reuse from prefix structure, recency, frequency, semantics, queued demand, or workload history [12, 22, 33, 34, 44, 46, 48, 49]. MeritKV, Bidaw, LRU-K, and NVIDIA KVBM further incorporate reuse utility, storage cost, or write awareness [14, 17, 30, 35]. Orthogonal tech-niques reduce the footprint through token eviction, quantization, or model-level KV compression [8, 25, 53]. Most of these techniques do not jointly manage DRAM placement, SSD admission, and SSD eviction using attention-type reuse heterogeneity and an explicit endurance cost. 

Flash-aware caching and storage. General flash caches control wear through admission filtering and write-rate constraints [10, 47, 51], while offline work extends Bélady-style analysis to account for flash erasures [6]. Device- and interface-level techniques reduce physical writes through retention management, stream placement, and cache-aware reclamation [24, 26, 27, 39]. For LLM serving, HiFC, FlashAccel, and HBFlex optimize flash data paths, writeback, and reclamation [15, 45, 54]. These mechanisms are complementary to Gandalf because they do not use attention semantics to value individual KV states. 

Lifetime-aware KV placement. Most closely related, Lachesis places agent KV segments across HBM and high-bandwidth flash using lifetimes inferred from the agent harness [50]. In contrast, Gandalf targets host DRAM and SSD, derives reuse differences from hybrid-attention semantics without harness-specific informa-tion, and jointly considers capacity, recomputation benefit, and SSD write cost. 

## 7 Conclusion 

This paper presents Gandalf , an SSD-based KV cache offloading system that uses the attention type associated with each KV cache object to predict its likelihood of future reuse. Gandalf retains short-lived linear-attention states in DRAM, while offloading full-attention KV caches with high reuse potential to SSD. Its placement policy balances the expected cache hits from each SSD write against the resulting device wear. By filtering writes based on both reuse potential and wear cost, Gandalf preserves cache hit rates while extending SSD lifetime. Across a range of hybrid models and hard-ware capacities, Gandalf improves SSD lifetime by 2–7.3 × over the best baseline. As KV caches continue to outgrow available memory, we hope Gandalf motivates future KV cache systems to consider not only the performance benefits of SSD offloading, but also the wear caused by each write. We leave the extension of Gandalf to other types of flash-based storage as a future work.

# ArchAgent: Agentic AI for Heterogeneous System Design Across Chip, Scale-Up, and Scale-Out Domains 

Murali Emani 

Argonne National Laboratory, IL, USA memani@anl.gov 

Kashyap Manjusha 

University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign, IL, USA kr58@ieee.org 

Abstract —The design space of modern AI/HPC systems has expanded rapidly across compute architectures, including GPUs, dataflow processors, and RISC-V accelerators; memory tech-nologies, including HBM, GDDR, and SRAM; and interconnects such as NVLink, UALink, Infinity Fabric, InfiniBand, Slingshot, and Ultra Ethernet. No single architecture is optimal across LLM training, LLM serving, and AI-coupled HPC workloads, while the resulting combinatorial design space is too large for manual exploration. We present ArchAgent, a hierarchical multi-agent framework in which an LLM-based planner decomposes system design into chip, scale-up, and scale-out subproblems, each handled by specialist agents. A deterministic analytical evaluator closes the loop, ensuring that all performance estimates are derived from analytical models rather than generated by the LLM. On a mixed AI+HPC workload under a 1 MW power budget, ArchAgent discovers heterogeneous designs that achieve a 1.93 × weighted geometric mean speedup over a homogeneous GPU baseline, while requiring only 18 evaluations compared with 100 evaluations for hill climbing, which achieves 1.65 × speedup. 

Index Terms —agentic AI, LLM agents, heterogeneous systems, design-space exploration, interconnects, co-design 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

The hardware substrate for large-scale AI and HPC has fragmented along three axes. Compute spans SIMT GPUs (NVIDIA H100/H200 and Blackwell B200/GB300; AMD In-stinct MI300X/MI325X/MI355X), matrix-engine ASICs with integrated networking (AWS Trainium2 [1]), systolic-array TPUs (Google TPU v5p, Trillium, and Ironwood [2]), SRAM-resident dataflow processors (Groq’s LPU [3], Cerebras’ wafer-scale WSE-3 [4]), reconfigurable dataflow units (Sam-baNova SN40L [5]), near-memory compute architectures (d-Matrix Corsair) and RISC-V native accelerators such as Ten-storrent’s Blackhole [6]. Memory spans stacked HBM (HBM2e through HBM3e, with HBM4 emerging), GDDR6/GDDR7, LPDDR5X coupled to host CPUs (e.g., Grace), on-chip SRAM ranging from hundreds of megabytes (LPU) to tens of gigabytes (WSE-3), tiered SRAM–HBM–DDR hierarchies (SN40L), and CXL-attached pooled memory [7]; these tiers differ by orders of magnitude in capacity, bandwidth, and cost per byte. Interconnects have fractured into proprietary and emerging scale-up domains (NVLink, AMD Infinity Fab-ric, Google’s ICI with optical circuit switching [2], AWS NeuronLink, and the open UALink [8] standard), Ethernet-native designs that blur the scale-up/scale-out boundary (Ten-storrent’s Ethernet mesh), and competing scale-out families (InfiniBand NDR/XDR, HPE Slingshot-11 [9], RoCEv2-based fabrics such as NVIDIA Spectrum-X, and Ultra Ethernet [10]). Together, these choices create a heterogeneous substrate in which compute architecture, memory hierarchy, and com-munication fabric are increasingly co-designed rather than independently selected. Workloads have diverged just as sharply. LLM training is dominated by GEMM and all-reduce operations, prefill is primarily compute-bound, decode is constrained by memory bandwidth, and AI-coupled HPC workloads alternate between FP64 solver phases and dense AI computation. As a result, no single chip–fabric combination performs well across all phases, motivating heterogeneous system compositions. How-ever, the resulting design space is highly combinatorial, span-ning chip types, partition sizes, per-partition interconnects, and workload assignments. This complexity makes the space difficult to navigate through human intuition alone, while conventional black-box auto-tuners often struggle because they lack the architectural priors needed to reason about these system-level trade-offs. LLM-based agents are well suited to this problem because they can leverage architectural priors when navigating the design space. For example, an agent can recognize that de-code is typically bandwidth-bound, identify SRAM-resident dataflow architectures as a natural fit, and independently propose prefill/decode disaggregation. The risk of hallucinated performance claims can be addressed structurally: all perfor-mance estimates are produced by a deterministic analytical evaluator invoked as a tool, rather than generated by the model itself. In this design, the LLM is responsible for reasoning over architectural choices, while the evaluator provides the quantitative performance evidence used to assess them. In this work, our primary contributions are: (1) ArchAgent ,a hierarchical multi-agent framework in which a planner decomposes system design into chip, scale-up, and scale-out subproblems, which are then handled by specialist sub-agents in a closed loop with a validating evaluator. (2) An open archetype library covering GPU, dataflow, and RISC-V compute architectures, together with ten fabric models and a calibrated analytical cost model based on a roofline chip model and α–β collective communication model, spanning LLM

II. R ELATED WORK 

a) Architecture DSE: Prior work has extensively ex-plored automated accelerator design-space exploration. Arch-Gym [11] uses RL, GAMMA [12] employs genetic search, and AutoDSE [13] applies bottleneck-guided search; related systems such as Timeloop/Accelergy [14] and MAESTRO [15] explore accelerator mappings and dataflows. These approaches generally treat the evaluator as a black box and optimize within a predefined parameter space, without explicitly reasoning about architectural relationships that transfer across workloads. 

ArchAgent , in contrast, uses an LLM to exploit architectural priors while coordinating system-level search. 

b) LLMs for hardware: Recent work applies LLMs to hardware development, including ChipNeMo [16] for chip-design workflows and SpecLLM [17] and RTL-oriented sys-tems [18], [19] for specification and RTL generation. These efforts primarily target individual chips or design artifacts; we instead consider full-system composition, jointly selecting compute architectures, scale-up fabrics, and scale-out net-works. 

c) Performance modeling: Our evaluator follows the analytical modeling tradition of Calculon [20] and LLMCom-pass [21] for transformer performance and ASTRA-sim [22] for hierarchical collective communication. We abstract intra-chip mapping, addressed by Timeloop/Accelergy [14], [23], through effective utilization factors and focus on system-level performance across heterogeneous compute and interconnects. To our knowledge, ArchAgent is the first framework to pair hierarchical LLM agents with a deterministic evaluator to jointly explore chip, scale-up, and scale-out design, auditable configurations together with the reasoning behind key design choices. III. T HE ARCH AGENT FRAMEWORK 

A. Design Representation B. Design Representation 

We represent each candidate system as a JSON document containing a list of partitions . Each partition specifies a chip archetype, a scale-up fabric, a scale-out fabric, a fraction of the facility power budget, and a workload role (general ,

prefill , decode , solver , or ai ). Workload roles allow the evaluator to model disaggregated execution without requir-ing the agent to explicitly manage placement. For example, a serving configuration can assign prefill to a GPU partition and decode to an SRAM-resident dataflow partition. Before any performance evaluation, a schema validator checks the design for basic feasibility, including fabric bandwidth that exceeds the chip’s available port capacity, power allocations that exceed the facility budget, and insufficient FP64 capability for HPC workloads. Validation errors are returned to the agent as structured feedback, preventing evaluation cycles from being spent on invalid or unsupported configurations. 

C. Agent Hierarchy 

The planner agent proposes and iteratively revises com-plete system designs. For each candidate, it must invoke the evaluate_design tool, which provides the planner with the hardware catalog, design schema, and optimization objective, but not with predicted performance improvements. Three specialist sub-agents provide additional guidance during the search: a chip agent reasons about compute archetypes, roofline constraints, and memory hierarchies; a scale-up agent applies α–β reasoning to intra-domain communication and domain sizing; and a scale-out agent analyzes collective com-munication and system topology. Their recommendations are returned as concise parameter-level guidance and attached to the corresponding evaluation result. The evaluator is a deterministic analytical model. Given a valid configuration, it returns the mixed-workload score, per-workload performance relative to the reference system, and an attribution of the dominant bottleneck among compute throughput, memory bandwidth, fabric latency, and bisection bandwidth. A key structural invariant is that the LLM agents never generate or assert performance estimates themselves; all performance quantities originate from the deterministic evaluator. This separation makes the search process auditable: the complete sequence of candidate designs, evaluation results, and agent recommendations can be recorded and inspected. 

D. Analytical Cost Model 

We model conventional accelerator performance using a roofline formulation with dtype-specific peak FLOP rates, machine-utilization factors (MFU), and memory-bandwidth efficiency factors. For dataflow architectures whose sharded weights fit within on-chip SRAM, we instead model pipelined layer sharding, accounting for per-stage computation and point-to-point activation transfers rather than tensor-parallel all-reduce. This captures the execution model of SRAM-resident architectures such as Groq and Cerebras. Communication costs are modeled using the standard α–β

formulation [24]. The model supports ring and hierarchical all-reduce, with communication first within a domain and then across domains, as well as all-to-all and point-to-point transfers. For LLM training, we combine tensor, pipeline, and data parallelism (TP/PP/DP)[25], including pipeline-bubble overhead and a ZeRO-style[26] memory-feasibility check. For serving, we model disaggregated prefill and decode [27] and assign each phase to partitions according to their workload roles. The HPC suite consists of four workloads chosen to exercise distinct compute and communication regimes: a 27-point FP64 stencil dominated by halo exchange, an HPCG-like conjugate-gradient (CG) kernel with latency-sensitive scalar all-reduce, a 3D FFT dominated by all-to-all communication and global bisection bandwidth, and a coupled AI-surrogate–solver loop in which successive phases can be pipelined across hetero-geneous partitions. Section IV-B describes calibration of the analytical model against published MLPerf results [28] and vendor-reported measurements. IV. E VALUATION

B. Model Calibration 

An automated verification suite comprising 32 checks vali-dates the model at three levels: specification invariants against vendor datasheets, asymptotic identities for collective com-munication models, and end-to-end performance predictions against published measurements. As representative calibration points, the model predicts 910 tok/s/GPU for BF16 training of Llama-70B on a 1,024-H100 cluster, consistent with the approximately 750–1,100 tok/s/GPU reported by MLPerf-class runs [28]. For FP8 decode at TP4 with batch size 32, the model predicts 109 tok/s/user, within the approximately 60– 150 tok/s/user range reported for TRT-LLM. For HPCG, the model produces iteration times on the millisecond scale, con-sistent with the memory-bandwidth-limited behavior reported for comparable systems.

Planner capability also affects search outcomes. Under identical evaluation budgets and guardrails, gemma-4-31B-it converges to a score of 0.87, below the homogeneous reference, and produces 7 schema-invalid and 4 otherwise infeasible designs in 50 evaluations. In comparison, gpt-oss-120b produces 5 schema-invalid and no infeasible designs under the same conditions. These results suggest that the framework can also serve as a controlled probe of how model capability affects architectural search, although the observed differences reflect the complete agent-and-model system rather than model capability alone. The hardware used to host the planner affects only search wall-clock time, as expected given the deterministic evaluator. Finally, the reported designs benefit in part from the gen-erational advantage of B200 over the H100 reference. The same-generation control experiment, reported in the artifact, isolates the contribution of architectural heterogeneity from this generational effect. 

D. Search Efficiency 

Fig. 3 shows convergence under a matched 100-evaluation budget. ArchAgent surpasses hill climbing’s final score of 1.65 by evaluation 18 and reaches its best score of 1.93 by approximately evaluation 36, after which no further improve-ment is observed. Random search requires 48 evaluations to exceed a score of 1.5. The faster convergence is consistent with the architectural structure encoded in the agent hierarchy: the planner’s early proposals already incorporate multi-partition configurations and workload-role assignments, whereas hill climbing must discover these structural choices through local mutations. 

E. Implications for Future Systems 

The remaining performance gaps in the winning config-urations consistently concentrate along two axes: all-to-all communication and FP64 memory bandwidth. In particular, FFT performance remains constrained by global bisection bandwidth, while the stencil workload is limited by FP64 memory throughput. These results suggest three directions for future mixed AI+HPC systems. (i) Scale-out fabrics would benefit from higher injection bandwidth, with ≥200GB/s per endpoint as a representative target, together with hardware support for in-network collective and all-to-all communication. (ii) Memory systems with HBM4-class bandwidth ( ≥10TB/s per socket), or accelerators that combine high memory band-width with substantial FP64 capability through dataflow or near-memory compute, could reduce the performance gap on bandwidth-bound HPC workloads. (iii) System procurement could be organized around workload roles rather than a single system. Across our search results, the optimizer frequently allocates 2–10% of the power budget to specialized decode or solver partitions, suggesting that a relatively small het-erogeneous component can complement a predominantly AI-oriented system and account for a substantial portion of the improvement over homogeneous configurations. V. D ISCUSSION AND LIMITATIONS 

Our conclusions are bounded by the fidelity of the analytical evaluator. The current model does not capture switch-level congestion, uses fixed factors for compute/communication overlap, and does not yet account for KV-cache transfer in disaggregated serving. These simplifications can affect the relative evaluation of communication-intensive configurations. The wafer-scale archetype is approximated as a set of compos-able slices, which may underestimate intra-wafer bandwidth and overestimate inter-slice communication performance. Sim-ilarly, the iso-cost analysis inherits uncertainty from publicly reported list prices. These limitations concern the evaluator rather than the framework: the agent loop is evaluator-agnostic, allowing the analytical model to be replaced or augmented by higher-fidelity models. As a next step, we plan to validate selected top-ranked configurations with simulator-in-the-loop evalua-tion, using ASTRA-sim [22] for collective communication and Timeloop [14]-class tools for chip-level mapping, while keeping such expensive evaluations outside the primary search loop. On the agent side, LLM stochasticity motivates reporting results across multiple random seeds. Search outcomes also depend on planner capability. We view this dependence as an experimentally useful property of the framework: because performance estimates are generated exclusively by the deter-ministic evaluator, differences in planning capability can be reflected directly in the quality of the resulting configurations under a fixed evaluation environment. This makes ArchAgent a potential benchmark for studying open-weight models on hardware-design search tasks. Ablations isolating the con-tributions of the specialist-agent hierarchy and bottleneck-attribution feedback remain future work. VI. C ONCLUSION 

In this paper, we present ArchAgent, a framework that com-bines hierarchical LLM agents with a deterministic evaluator to explore heterogeneous system designs. Our results show that this approach can search complex architectural design spaces more efficiently than stochastic baselines while identifying higher-quality configurations. Under a mixed AI+HPC work-load profile and an iso-power constraint, ArchAgent achieves a 1.93 × improvement over the homogeneous GPU reference and reaches a design quality exceeding hill climbing’s final result within 18 evaluations. The configurations discovered by ArchAgent also exhibit a consistent architectural pattern: a large GPU-like partition complemented by smaller, role-specialized dataflow and FP64 partitions. This result suggests that future computing facilities may benefit from composing hardware around workload roles rather than relying exclusively on homogeneous accelerator fleets. At the component level, the remaining performance bottlenecks highlight two capabilities that are less prominent in current AI-oriented accelerators: global bisection bandwidth and FP64 memory bandwidth. These results point to both capabilities as promising directions for future accelerator and system design. VII. A CKNOWLEDGEMENT

E. Role Labels vs. Realized Placement 

The role assigned by the agent does not necessarily de-termine the partition used during execution. In Iter33 , the wafer-scale partition is labeled decode , but the evaluator assigns both serving stages to the B200 partition. With only four wafer slices available, the 70B FP8 model cannot be accommodated in on-slice SRAM: sharding requires 35,GB per slice, compared with 5.5,GB of available SRAM. Conse-quently, SRAM-resident decode is not feasible at this budget, and the evaluator selects the B200 partition, which provides the required capacity through HBM3e. The WSE-3 partition is instead used for the AI-surrogate phase of the coupled AI +HPC workload. For the FNO proxy, its dense FP16 throughput is higher than the effective through-put of the B200 partition, and pipelining the AI and solver phases produces a 2.61 × coupled speedup. Thus, role labels serve as guidance to the workload map-per rather than as hard placement constraints. The realized mapping reported in Table III reflects the actual execution configuration, while both the agent-specified roles and the resulting placement can be reconstructed from the audit trail. At larger power budgets ( ≥ 8 wafer slices per replica), SRAM-resident decode becomes feasible, causing the decode role and the realized placement to coincide.

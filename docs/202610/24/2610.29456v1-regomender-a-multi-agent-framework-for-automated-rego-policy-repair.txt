## Abstract 

Policy-as-Code (PaC) has emerged as a foundational paradigm in cloud-native systems. However, repairing Rego policies remains challenging due to their strict syntax, declarative semantics, and spe-cific verification requirements. Existing automated program repair (APR) methods are mainly designed for mainstream languages and struggle to accommodate the unique policy logic and repair work-flows of Rego. Thus, Rego policy repair remains a manual process driven by trial and error. To address this, we introduce RegoBench, the first automated Rego policy benchmark comprising 1,159 real-world defects, and propose RegoMender, a feedback-guided multi-agent framework. Collaborating via a shared blackboard and an atomic toolkit, RegoMender dynamically executes patching, rewrit-ing, or rolling back, to resolve complex repair scenarios. Evaluated on RegoBench, RegoMender achieves syntactic and test-based se-mantic success rates of 82.9% and 77.9%, respectively, outperforming the strongest baseline by 13.4%. ## 1 Introduction 

Policy-as-Code has become a core paradigm for enforcing security and compliance requirements in cloud-native systems. It represents policies as executable rules that are continuously evaluated against structured artifacts, such as API requests, configuration objects, and arXiv:2610.29456v1 [cs.SE] 21 Oct 2026 ASE ’26, October 12–16, 2026, Munich, Germany Ruijie Zhang, Huaxiao Yin, Li Hu, Fanqi Kong, Haozhe Liang, Shaoyin Cheng, Kun Zhang, and Bibo Tu 

infrastructure-as-code (IaC) plans, across both delivery and runtime environments. A widely adopted implementation of PaC is Open Policy Agent (OPA), which separates policy logic from application code and provides a unified evaluation framework across heteroge-neous platforms. In practice, OPA is integrated into CI/CD pipelines for pre-deployment validation and into Kubernetes admission con-trol to prevent non-compliant resource updates. OPA policies are written in Rego, a declarative language designed to express policy rules over inputs and produce authorization decisions [39]. However, repairing Rego policies is particularly challenging be-cause of their declarative semantics and strict validation require-ments. Unlike imperative languages, Rego follows a logic program-ming paradigm derived from Datalog, where unification combines pattern matching with variable binding. This context sensitivity means that even a seemingly simple modification may unintention-ally alter the logic of the policy. Furthermore, policy evaluation may return undefined when certain data paths are missing. Rather than raising an error, this result may cause conditions to be skipped. Meanwhile, the current APR benchmarks [26, 27, 48, 57] have been developed primarily for mainstream programming languages and rely on test suites as correctness oracles. Classic datasets such as Defects4J [27] provide standardized collections of real-world Java bugs with regression tests, while repository-level benchmarks like SWE-bench [26] evaluate issue resolution in large-scale Python repositories, collectively advancing research in APR. However, ded-icated benchmarks for evaluating the automated repair of Rego policies are absent. Unlike conventional programs, policies written in Rego are validated through a tool-driven workflow that includes linting and compilation, policy-specific testing, and compliance checks derived from domain requirements. This mismatch presents two fundamental challenges for the au-tomated repair of Rego policies: (i) Lack of benchmarks for Rego repair. To date, there is no publicly available dataset specifically designed for Rego policy repair, making it difficult to systemat-ically evaluate whether existing APR or LLM-based approaches can effectively handle Rego-specific syntax, semantics, and veri-fication workflows. (ii) Heavy reliance on manual or ad hoc LLM-based repair workflows. In practice, Rego policy repair still relies heavily on manual debugging by domain experts or on ad hoc interactions with LLMs , developers must manually interpret policy failures, construct prompts, invoke opa check and opa test , assess the verification outcomes, and iteratively revise candidate patches. This human-in-the-loop repair process is time-consuming, error-prone, and difficult to scale, especially when repairs must preserve repository-specific policy intent and compliance semantics. To address the lack of systematic data and reproducible evalua-tion for Rego policy repair, we mine real-world defect fixes from 2,847 GitHub repositories that actively maintain Rego policies. We then design a strict three-stage filtering pipeline to progressively refine the raw commit-level candidates, including isolated patch extraction, redundancy and noise mitigation, and deterministic ver-ification under a standardized OPA toolchain. This process yields 1,159 high-quality buggy-fix pairs that are fully reproducible. This benchmark covers seven OPA-derived defect types, preserves the natural difficulty distribution of real-world defects and features a high proportion of complex chained repairs, directly measuring how well repair methods handle practical programming tasks. Furthermore, we design RegoMender, a multi-agent framework for automated Rego policy repair. RegoMender establishes a closed loop between code repair and OPA verification by coordinating three specialized roles, namely the Orchestrator, Analyzer, and Re-pairer, through a shared Blackboard and an auditable atomic toolkit. Specifically, all repository interactions are carried out through the atomic toolkit, which provides five core operations: Verify ,

Context Scan , Error Triage , Rewrite , and Patch . This design ensures that each repair step remains precise and reproducible. Guided by real-time verifier feedback, the Orchestrator coordinates the Analyzer and Repairer, records progress on the Blackboard, and rolls back when necessary to avoid stagnation. In this way, Re-goMender supports reliable multi-step repair, particularly in cases where resolving one failure exposes additional hidden defects. To evaluate the effectiveness of RegoMender, we conduct exten-sive experiments on RegoBench. First, the results show that, com-pared to existing automated program repair methods [5, 20, 53, 58], RegoMender achieves syntactic and test-based semantic success rates of 82.9% and 77.9%, respectively. It ranks first across all seven defect types, demonstrating the effectiveness of our agent orches-tration and task decision-making mechanisms. Second, we analyze the impact of different backbone models on RegoMender and the baselines. We find that RegoMender demonstrates superior robust-ness across different models , and DeepSeek-V3.1-Terminus [34] provides the best balance between performance and cost. Third, we conduct a comparative study on single-step and chained repair tasks. We find that RegoMender maintains stable performance in both situations. Furthermore, we conduct an ablation study on the designed atomic toolkit. The results reveal that the Context Scan 

tool has the most significant impact on the framework’s perfor-mance. This indicates that the repair process for the Rego policies relies on context gathering . To evaluate its cross-language general-izability, we extend our method to two additional domain-specific languages (HCL and SQL). The results show that RegoMender con-tinues to maintain a significant advantage over other baselines. Finally, to verify its practical utility in the real world, we evaluate our method on 132 CVE vulnerabilities. RegoMender achieves a repair rate of 86.4%, outperforming ThinkRepair and RepairAgent and demonstrating its practical potential for security-related repair. In summary, our contributions are as follows: 

• To the best of our knowledge, this is the first work to explore automated repair of Rego policies, laying the groundwork for systematic and reproducible research in this area. 

• We introduce RegoBench, the first benchmark for automated Rego policy repair. To construct it, we design a three-stage filtering pipeline and apply it to 2,847 GitHub repositories that actively maintain Rego policies, curating a high-quality dataset of 1,159 real-world defects. 

• We propose RegoMender, a multi-agent framework where Orchestrator, Analyzer, and Repairer agents collaborate via a Blackboard and an auditable toolkit to achieve Rego repairs with rollback-based stagnation control. 

• Extensive evaluations demonstrate that RegoMender achieves state-of-the-art test-based semantic success rates of 77.9%, outperforming the strongest baseline by 13.4%. Notably, it exhibits exceptional stability in complex multi-step defects and successfully resolves 86.4% of real-world CVEs. ## 2 Motivation 

Figure 1 shows a motivating example of Rego policy repair. In Figure 1(a), the buggy rule uses unification ( =) and directly reads 

input.request.object.metadata.labels after checking that the resource kind is "Pod" . Under Rego’s undefined-value semantics, if the labels field is absent or the binding fails, the rule body may become undefined , so the intended deny decision is not produced. In Figure 1(b), the repair uses explicit comparison ( == ) for the kind check, retrieves labels with object.get(input...metadata, "labels", ) , and uses strict assignment ( := ) for derived values. These changes handle missing labels explicitly and restore a well-defined admission decision. This example shows why Rego repair is language-specific. The operators =, := , and == represent unifi-cation, assignment, and comparison rather than simple symbol replacements, and whether an input path is defined depends on the runtime context. Therefore, Rego repairs should be validated with the standard OPA toolchain ( opa check and opa test ) to avoid de-ployment failures. Current LLMs also have limited experience with Rego repair because repair data remains scarce, making reliable decisions from prior patterns difficult. Based on the above analysis, we construct RegoBench, a bench-mark of 1,159 Rego repair instances covering a broad range of syn-tax and semantic errors, and propose RegoMender, a multi-agent framework for automated Rego policy repair. Recent software en-gineering systems use specialized agents for planning, analysis, coding, and tool use [18, 19, 45, 50]. However, such collaboration does not directly address Rego-specific needs in policy semantics, context extraction, and OPA-based verification. To address these needs, our framework coordinates three specialized agents via a shared toolkit and blackboard for automated Rego policy repair. 

## 3 Overview 

In this section, we first describe the construction of the proposed benchmark, RegoBench (§3.1), followed by a detailed presentation of our multi-agent repair framework, RegoMender (§3.2). 

## 3.1 RegoBench Construction 

We construct RegoBench, the first benchmark dedicated to real-world Rego defects, follows three main steps. First, we define each repair instance and the evaluation criteria for a successful repair. Next, we execute the data filtering process to collect high-quality instances from open-source repositories. Finally, we classify the benchmark along several characteristic dimensions to provide a comprehensive overview of the dataset. 

3.1.1 Instance Definition. We define each repair instance through four core components that encapsulate all necessary context, en-suring each defect is self-contained and independently verifiable: 

• Code Pair. We store two versions of the policy code: 𝐶 𝑏𝑢𝑔 and 

𝐶 𝑓 𝑖𝑥 . 𝐶 𝑏𝑢𝑔 represents the defective policy, while 𝐶 𝑓 𝑖𝑥 is the corresponding manual fix. Alongside the raw code, we record metadata, such as the commit hash, repository link, and rela-tive file paths. This information enables the precise historical code state to be retrieved and replayed deterministically. 

• Controlled Context. This component includes dependency resolution results, the module import graph, and generated code stubs. These elements allow for isolated verification, meaning the policy can be evaluated locally without the need to download or configure the entire repository. 

• Standardized Toolchain. We enforce a fixed OPA version (v1.10.1) and consistent command line arguments. This ensures consistent diagnostic messages and removes any variability that might arise from using different tool versions. 

• Two-Tier Oracle Records. We organize verification into two tiers. The first ensures syntactic correctness via opa check ,covering syntax errors, linking issues, type mismatches, and safety violations. The second ensures semantic correctness via 

opa test to prevent logical regressions. To qualify as a valid repair instance, the code must pass both tiers, guaranteeing that every sample is syntactically and behaviorally correct. 

3.1.2 Data Filtering. To construct the initial corpus, we query the GitHub Search API for repositories containing .rego files. To ensure data quality and relevance, we apply a strict activity filter, re-taining only repositories with at least 40 commits between January 1, 2020, and June 30, 2024. This yields 2,847 qualified repositories. We then analyze their commit histories to extract potential defect re-pairs, resulting in 54,821 raw candidates. However, extracting these defects from open source repositories introduces noise. Specifically, we observe four patterns of invalid repairs. First, superficial fixes occur when commits labeled as repairs consist only of formatting changes or code refactoring. Fourth, environmental instability arises when failures depend on external variables or continuous integra-tion contexts that are difficult to reproduce. We therefore design a three-stage filtering pipeline to obtain a high-quality dataset. 

1) Isolated Patch Extraction. To resolve coupling across files, we enforce a strict single-file pairing rule. Only commits with substan-tive edits confined to a single .rego file are retained, ensuring a clear mapping between the defective and fixed states at the same file path. Nonfunctional edits are discarded to ensure each instance represents a meaningful repair. This filtering step reduces the raw corpus from 54,821 to 8,643 candidates. 

2) Redundancy & Noise Mitigation. To reduce noise from repeti-tive patches, we implement a clear deduplication strategy. We first exclude forked and archived repositories and then use file finger-prints, defined by the repository, commit hash, and file path, to prevent redundant processing. Additionally, we apply content hash-ing to eliminate identical code copies caused by version control operations. Finally, we manually audit the dataset to discard sam-ples where the link between the code change and the verification result is unclear. Through this process, we retain 3,071 candidates. 

3) Two-Tier Oracle Verification. Finally, to ensure reproducibility and eliminate environmental inconsistencies, we establish a de-terministic two-tier verification process using the standard OPA toolchain. Each retained instance must satisfy strict evaluation cri-teria. First, the defective policy must trigger a verifiable failure (via either opa check or opa test ) to confirm the presence of a bug. Second, the developer fix must successfully pass both verification tiers. This process guarantees that every accepted instance is both syntactically valid and behaviorally correct. Following this three-stage filtering pipeline, we refine the raw data into a dataset of 1,159 high-quality and reproducible instances.

To characterize this dataset, we classify the repair difficulty into three levels: easy, medium, and hard. ## 3.2 RegoMender Framework 

RegoMender is an automated multi-agent framework designed for repairing Rego policies. As shown in Figure 2, our approach cen-ters on an Orchestrator that manages a continuous feedback loop driven by the results of opa check and opa test . The Orchestrator RegoMender: A Multi-agent Framework for Automated Rego Policy Repair ASE ’26, October 12–16, 2026, Munich, Germany 

coordinates two specialized agents, namely the Analyzer and the Repairer, using a shared blackboard to exchange state information. To make precise code changes and prevent the repair process from getting stuck, RegoMender includes an atomic repair toolkit, as well as a mechanism for stagnation detection and rollback. We detail these core components and the overall execution workflow below. 

3.2.1 Components. Each part RegoMender takes on a distinct role to ensure a smooth repair process. We outline their specific tasks and internal logic below. 

Atomic Repair Toolkit. To make the system behavior auditable and reproducible, we abstract all operations into five atomic tools. These tools serve as the sole interface for the agents: 

• Verify: Provides the system source of truth by running syntax and semantic checks to return a formal set of errors. 

• Context Scan: Extracts an abstract syntax tree and a depen-dency graph from the current policy to help agents understand the available variables and packages. 

• Error Triage: Converts raw error information into a struc-tured format containing the error category, line location, and the specific message associated with each line. 

• Patch: Conducts corrections on individual code lines, such as modifying a variable name, flipping a logical operator, or refining a specific policy constraint. 

• Rewrite: Replaces entire policy blocks or regenerates complex logic when simple edits are insufficient. 

Blackboard. To reduce context loss in multi-agent collaboration, RegoMender uses a structured shared blackboard as the central repository for the system state. This component contains three fields: (i) the Code Field, which maintains a versioned history of the Rego code, allowing the system to retrieve previous code snap-shots for rollback; (ii) the Report Field, which stores the sequence of structured diagnostic reports generated by the Analyzer, includ-ing the error category, line location, corresponding error message, relevant context information extracted by the Context Scan tool, and a priority value to guide the Repairer in fixing critical issues first; and (iii) the Error Signature Field, which maintains a tuple 

⟨Error Category , Error Count , Error Streak ⟩. We define the first two elements as a state (𝑠𝑡𝑎𝑡𝑒 = ⟨Error Category , Error Count ⟩) , which serves as the main signal for agent routing. The Error Streak then records the number of consecutive occurrences of the same 𝑠𝑡𝑎𝑡𝑒 .This allows the Orchestrator to monitor the persistence of failure patterns, enabling it to trigger rollbacks or strategy adjustments. 

Orchestrator. The Orchestrator serves as the decision-maker and quality controller of RegoMender. It does not generate code directly but coordinates the workflow by dispatching tasks to the Ana-lyzer or the Repairer. Its strategy is determined by a decision rule 

𝜋 (next | state ), where next ∈ { Analyzer , Repairer , Rollback , Done }

and state captures the current error category and count. In each iter-ation, the Orchestrator retrieves the latest code from the Blackboard and invokes the Verify tool. If verification fails, the Orchestrator updates the Error Signature and selects the next action based on the current repair state and explicit routing conditions defined in its prompt. Localized failures with clear line-level diagnostics are treated as simple errors and sent directly to the Repairer. Am-biguous, dependency-related, or repeatedly unresolved failures are treated as complex errors and sent to the Analyzer for additional context and dependency analysis before repair. After each repair, the Orchestrator invokes Verify again. A changed state indicates progress and resets the Error Streak, whereas an unchanged state triggers a revised diagnosis from the Analyzer. Once the predefined stagnation threshold is reached, the Orchestrator rolls back to the snapshot saved before the stalled attempts and requests a new diag-nosis. The workflow concludes as Done only after the policy passes both syntactic and semantic validation. To prevent infinite loops, the Orchestrator monitors the repair progress and stops the process if the maximum iteration limit is reached. 

Analyzer. The Analyzer acts as the diagnostic specialist, engaged by the Orchestrator when error messages are too complex or am-biguous for direct repair. Upon receiving this task, it retrieves the latest error stream and uses the Error Triage tool to extract a structured error description, mapping each error to its category, line location, and corresponding message. At the same time, the 

Context Scan tool extracts context and dependency information for the identified code region. The Analyzer then analyzes each error to infer its underlying cause and synthesizes these findings into a diagnostic report written to the shared blackboard. 

Repairer. As the execution specialist, the Repairer modifies the Rego code based on the diagnostic report retrieved from the Black-board. It uses Rewrite tool for normalization or a high volume of structural issues (i.e., more than 10 errors), and Patch tool for localized faults. The resulting patch list specifies the affected lines and edits, after which the repaired code is synchronized back to the Blackboard for the next verification round. 

Stagnation Detection and Rollback Mechanism. To prevent infinite loops caused by complex dependency chains, the Orchestrator con-tinuously monitors repair progress. In our framework, stagnation is defined as a state where no meaningful progress is made. Specifi-cally, we consider the repair progress stalled if the Error Signature remains unchanged for 5 consecutive iterations ( 𝜏 = 5). When this happens, the Orchestrator triggers a rollback by retrieving the Rego code snapshot from the Blackboard that existed before the stagna-tion began. It then dispatches the Analyzer to review the historical reports stored on the Blackboard and reflect on why the previous attempts failed. Instead of a standard analysis, the Analyzer re-evaluates the persistent errors and their dependencies to issue a revised diagnostic report. This allows the Repairer to change its approach and try a different repair path to find a successful solution. 

3.2.2 Workflow. The Orchestrator manages RegoMender as a feedback loop for repairing a faulty Rego repository. It extracts the initial code, stores it in the Code Field of the Blackboard, and invokes the Verify tool on the latest code in each iteration for syntactic and semantic testing. If all tests pass, the workflow terminates as 

Done . Otherwise, the Orchestrator updates the Error Signature Field and selects the next action based on the current 𝑠𝑡𝑎𝑡𝑒 . For simple errors, it dispatches the Repairer to use the Patch or Rewrite tool; for complex errors, it invokes the Analyzer to synthesize raw logs and dependency context into the Report Field. To prevent infinite loops, the Orchestrator tracks the Error Streak . If the 𝑠𝑡𝑎𝑡𝑒 remains unchanged for 5 iterations, it rolls back to the snapshot saved prior to the stagnation. The Analyzer then reviews prior reports, revises the diagnostic strategy, and provides fresh guidance to the Repairer. ## 4 Experimental Setup 

We design a series of experiments to evaluate the effectiveness and efficiency of RegoMender. Our study is guided by the following 

Research Questions (RQs): 

• RQ1: How effective is RegoMender in repairing Rego policies? 

• RQ2: How do different LLM backbones impact the performance of RegoMender? 

• RQ3: How does RegoMender utilize collaborative tool orchestra-tion to resolve chained repairs? 

• RQ4: How does each atomic tool contribute to RegoMender? 

• RQ5: To what extent does RegoMender generalize to domain-specific language similar to Rego? 

• RQ6: Can RegoMender repair vulnerabilities in real-world de-ployment scenarios? 

## 4.1 Baselines 

Since automated Rego policy repair remains unexplored, we modify several existing automated repair methods [5, 19, 20, 50, 53, 58] to make them applicable to our study. We categorize them into standalone repair methods and general-purpose agent systems. 

Standalone Repair Methods. This category includes state-of-the-art (SOTA) automated program repair work tailored for LLMs. Specifically, Agentless [53] is a lightweight approach that avoids complex agent loops by using a strict two-phase pipeline of fault localization and patch generation. ThinkRepair [58] enhances patch quality by prompting the model to generate a structured thought process before outputting code. APR-MCTS [20] integrates LLMs with Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS) [6] to explore possi-ble patches using test feedback as reward signals, while RepairA-gent [5] formulates bug fixing as an interactive task where the LLM autonomously gathers context to construct a repair. 

General-Purpose Agent Systems. This category covers general-purpose agent systems designed for repository-level software engi-neering rather than specifically for automated program repair. It covers both multi-agent strategies and off-the-shelf coding agents. AutoDev [50] uses a conversation manager to coordinate repository-level tasks and execute commands, while MetaGPT [19] assigns specialized roles to agents to support collaboration similar to that of a software development team. Claude Code [3] and Codex [41] are off-the-shelf coding agents that can inspect repository context, modify source files, and execute development tools. We evaluate both coding agents through their official SDKs. We evaluate all baselines in RQ1. To manage costs, RQ2–RQ6 fo-cus on two representative baselines: ThinkRepair and RepairAgent. On Rego, as defects typically require repository-specific reasoning (e.g., cross-file de-pendencies), RegoMender improved over RepairAgent (77.9% vs. 64.5%), consistent with our main findings. On HCL, RegoMender still outperforms RepairAgent (82.3% vs. 78.2%), suggesting that orchestration remains helpful when oracle feedback is relatively precise but the repair space is still more complex than SQL. On SQL, as defects are mostly lightweight parse failures and the oracle is simpler, both methods performed identically (84.0%). 

Answer to RQ5: Overall, our results suggest that generalizabil-ity depends on DSL characteristics. Orchestration methods are most effective when repair tasks rely on multi-step reasoning and feedback localization is ambiguous. 

## 5.6 RQ6: Real-world Vulnerability Repair 

To assess reliability in security critical scenarios, we evaluate the ThinkRepair, the RepairAgent, and RegoMender on 132 real-world vulnerabilities. We construct this specific subset by filtering RegoB-ench for instances linked to known CVE records. Table 6 presents repair performance across different Common Weakness Enumerations (CWEs). The results highlight that direct prompting is insufficient for security patches, solving only 26.6%. However, agentic workflows significantly improve these results. Compared to the 67.6% success rate of the RepairAgent, our method reaches 86.4%, delivering a clear 18.8% overall gain. Breaking down this overall improvement by vulnerability type reveals two key trends. First, RegoMender shows consistent improvements across major categories. It outperforms the RepairAgent by 15.6% in Ac-cess Control and 19.3% in Privilege Management. This advantage expands to 20.0% for Incorrect Authorization due to complex logic. Second, our method proves robust on diverse cases. It achieves a 40.0% performance boost in the Other category. This result confirms that a multi-agent system handles diverse vulnerability types much more effectively than a single agent. 

Answer to RQ6: RegoMender significantly outperforms base-lines in repairing real-world vulnerabilities, achieving 86.4% success rate. More importantly, it is more reliable than RepairA-gent when dealing with diverse and mixed vulnerability types. 

## 6 Discussion 6.1 Failure Analysis 

To better understand the limitations of our approach, we present a qualitative analysis of failure instances where all evaluated methods failed to produce a valid repair. 

Unreachable Context. Failures often occur when project depen-dencies cannot be fully resolved. In complex projects, a file might rely on private modules, external code, or dynamically generated files. Since static analysis tools cannot access these missing parts, they provide an incomplete context. To fill these gaps, the model generates references or functions that look correct but are invalid. This keeps the system stuck in a loop of failed repair attempts, showing a limitation of relying solely on static analysis. 

Iteration Limitation. Beyond incomplete context, strict limits on repair iterations present another major hurdle. When a task involves multiple linked errors, fixing one bug often exposes an-other, creating a chain of issues that requires many attempts to fully resolve. Given the need to control costs, we set a maximum iteration limit, which can cause the RegoMender repair process to end just before the code is verified. Consequently, successfully re-pairing deeply interconnected errors under a strict iteration budget remains a major challenge. 

Semantic Ambiguity. Finally, failures can arise from coding conventions rather than simple syntax errors. A policy can be implemented in multiple valid ways, but only specific patterns align with the repository’s established style, such as the choice between 

deny and allow layouts. Without clear examples, the model may generate a patch that is technically correct but fails strict stylistic tests or is rejected during human review. 

## 6.2 Threats to Validity 

Internal Validity. One primary threat is that a repair may satisfy 

opa check and opa test while failing to fully preserve the original policy intent, particularly when test coverage is incomplete and misses edge cases. Another threat involves potential noise or data contamination in mined repair data, as commit histories may in-clude refactoring or deletions unrelated to bug fixing. We mitigate these threats in two ways. First, we construct RegoBench using a rigorous three-stage filtering pipeline and a fixed OPA toolchain to ensure each instance is reproducible and evaluated against na-tive verification signals. Second, to strengthen semantic evalua-tion beyond the available tests, we combine two-tier verification and regression monitoring with JudgePass, which examines Tier-2-passing repairs for unintended changes to policy behavior. We further assess the reliability of JudgePass against expert labels on a stratified sample. The close agreement between Pass@Tier-2 and JudgePass, together with the lower regression rate of RegoMender, provides complementary evidence of semantic preservation and RegoMender: A Multi-agent Framework for Automated Rego Policy Repair ASE ’26, October 12–16, 2026, Munich, Germany 

repair stability. Nevertheless, incomplete tests and specifications may still leave some semantic differences undetected. 

External Validity. A main external threat is that our study is cen-tered on Rego, so the findings may not transfer equally well to other Policy-as-Code settings or to simpler DSLs with different oracle behavior. Another practical deployment threat is that real envi-ronments may involve different model choices, budget constraints, and security-sensitive scenarios. We partially address these con-cerns through broader evaluation. Across different LLM backbones, RegoMender consistently improves repair performance, including on smaller open-source models. Beyond Rego, the cross-DSL re-sults on HCL and SQL suggest that the value of orchestration is not limited to one language, though the gain is larger when the repair requires multi-step reasoning. Finally, on 132 CVE-linked real-world vulnerability cases, RegoMender achieves strong results and clearly outperforms baselines. These findings support the prac-tical relevance of our method, although further validation on more languages and production settings is still needed. 

## 7 Related Work 7.1 Automated Program Repair 

Automated program repair has emerged as a promising technique to alleviate the manual debugging burden by automatically generat-ing patches for software defects. Traditional APR methods typically rely on test suites as specifications and can be broadly catego-rized into search-based and constraint-based approaches. Search-based methods, such as GenProg [32] and SimFix [25], formulate patch generation as a search problem over a syntactic mutation space. Conversely, constraint-based approaches, like SemFix [38] and Angelix [36], leverage symbolic execution to extract program semantics and synthesize patches via constraint solving. While these traditional methods have demonstrated empirical success on general-purpose programming languages like Java and C, their efficacy is often bottlenecked by the exponential search space and the scalability limitations of underlying solvers. In recent years, the rapid advancement of deep learning has catalyzed a paradigm shift towards learning-based APR. Early data-driven approaches [8, 15, 35, 51] treated patch generation as a Neu-ral Machine Translation (NMT) task, learning bug-fixing patterns from large-scale historical commits. More recently, large language models pre-trained on massive code corpora have exhibited remark-able capabilities in understanding code context and generating plau-sible patches through methods like few-shot prompting [28, 54, 58] or fine-tuning [33]. These learning-based models have significantly improved the naturalness and correctness of generated patches, pushing the boundaries of automated debugging in modern soft-ware development [11]. Despite these improvements, existing APR methods primarily focus on general-purpose languages like Java or Python, leaving a significant gap in the realm of domain-specific languages and declarative policies. Recent studies have explored related tasks in infrastructure repair and policy generation. InfraFix [48] re-pairs IaC scripts using verification rules designed for infrastruc-ture configurations rather than Rego policies. ARPaCCino [47] generates Rego policies from natural-language requirements to address Terraform violations, while Prose2Policy [16] translates natural-language access-control policies into executable Rego. Over-all, these systems cover IaC repair and Rego synthesis, but do not address repository-level repair of existing faulty Rego policies us-ing the OPA toolchain. Therefore, advancing APR in this domain requires novel frameworks designed specifically to capture the underlying logic and structural constraints of declarative policies. 

## 7.2 Multi-agent System 

Multi-agent Systems (MAS) have shown strong potential in soft-ware engineering [4, 5, 21–23, 43] by decomposing complex task into collaborative workflows [18]. Existing systems generally follow two main design paradigms. The first is role-based collaboration, where agents are assigned specialized responsibilities resembling human development teams, such as planning, coding, and review-ing, as exemplified by systems like ChatDev [45] and MetaGPT [19], which aim to improve modularity and coordination in large-scale development workflows. The second paradigm focuses on tool-integrated execution, where agents operate in sandboxed environ-ments and interact directly with external tools and verification infrastructure to support end-to-end task execution [50, 52, 56, 59]. In the context of program repair, MAS naturally enables a di-vision of labor by separating key stages such as fault localization, patch generation, and regression testing. It is often combined with search strategies to handle noisy or ambiguous feedback. However, existing MAS-based repair approaches still face significant chal-lenges, particularly in terms of agent coordination and integration with external verifiers [7, 30, 53]. These challenges are even more pronounced in verification-intensive domains such as Policy-as-Code. In such cases, using MAS to separate diagnosis from oracle execution proves especially beneficial, as it enables a more auditable and reproducible repair loop, while also mitigating the brittleness commonly associated with Rego policy repair [39, 47]. 

## 8 Conclusion and Future Work 

In this paper, we address the challenge of applying LLM-based repair to the strict reliability requirements of cloud-native Policy-as-Code. We focus on automated Rego repair, where success requires passing a rigorous verification toolchain rather than merely generating plau-sible code. Our experiments show that RegoMender consistently outperforms standalone baselines and general-purpose agent sys-tems, especially on complex, multi-step defects and security-critical issues, while reducing the risk of introducing new bugs. These results suggest that, for domain-specific language repair, system architecture can be more important than model capability alone, especially when it effectively uses repository context and verifier feedback. RegoBench and RegoMender provide a foundation for building reliable autonomous DevSecOps agents. Building on these findings, several directions merit exploration. First, expanding RegoBench to other Policy-as-Code languages would help assess the generality of our approach. Second, because production policies often depend on external modules and dynamic cluster states, combining static analysis with sandboxed execution could capture these dependencies and improve repair accuracy. Third, syntactically valid patches may still weaken security; auto-mated negative tests and intent-preservation checks could prevent such repairs. Finally, future work should assess the cost–benefit trade-off of multi-agent collaboration for practical deployment.

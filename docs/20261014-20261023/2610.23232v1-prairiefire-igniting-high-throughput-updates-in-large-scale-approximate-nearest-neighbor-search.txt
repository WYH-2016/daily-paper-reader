ABSTRACT
Arrays of modern NVMe SSDs are approaching memory in terms
of bandwidth in a single server while offering much lower cost
per gigabyte. Hence, approximate nearest neighbor search (ANNS)
for billion-scale vectors in applications such as recommendation,
search, and retrieval-augmented generation increasingly runs
on SSD-based systems. Unfortunately, due to the limited update
throughput of existing SSD-based solutions, the indexed corpus
cannot keep pace with continuous modifications to data and
embedding models, thus hindering broader deployment across
more scenarios and larger scales.
The bottleneck lies in CPU-intensive update paths, where fore-
ground insertions incur frequent SSD accesses and background
reassignments repeatedly relocate each vector, making high up-
date throughput difficult to achieve even by exhausting available
hardware. In this paper, using buffered updates and batched local
rebuilds, we develop PrairieFire with cascaded adjustments, con-
currency control, and CPU-GPU-SSD cooperation. These co-designs
avoid frequent SSD accesses in foreground updates and heavy back-
ground reassignments, achieving high update throughput while
maintaining excellent search performance.
Evaluations show that, compared with state-of-the-art systems,
PrairieFire can sustain ∼3-35× update throughput while main-
taining ∼3-5× search throughput simultaneously. It also achieves
∼1.5-8.7× cost efficiency. PrairieFire has been deployed in Red-
Note’s production environments for two months, improving busi-
ness metrics (e.g., click-through rate) by higher freshness without
frequent index rebuilds.

INTRODUCTION
Approximate nearest neighbor search (ANNS) over embedding
vectors underpins online services such as recommendation, search
engines, advertising, and retrieval-augmented generation [34, 35,
36, 38, 39]. To reduce storage cost over billions of vectors, recent
systems try to place raw vectors as cluster lists or as graph nodes
with neighboring edges from DRAM onto NVMe SSDs [17, 23, 29,
57, 62]. The corpus of vectors, however, is anything but static. In
upstream applications, items are continuously created and retired,
and the underlying embedding models are continuously adjusted
and synchronized at an hourly or finer cadence to track shifting
user behavior for better business outcomes [38, 42]. A practical
ANNS service must therefore find top-𝑘results against the latest
valid data while continuously absorbing a stream of inserts and
deletes arising from both dynamic items [24, 64] and continual
learning of embedding models [22, 69].
To support updates, based on existing static ANNS [17, 57],
two notable solutions have been proposed (i.e., OdinANN [24]
and SPFresh [64]). OdinANN follows the graph structure [57] and
introduces direct modifications to avoid the inefficiency of peri-
odic merging (e.g., 1.5-2× search latency and ∼3× peak memory
usage during merging [55, 67]). SPFresh adopts a partition-based
structure, which routes vectors to partitions through an in-memory
centroid index and maintains partitions’ lists on SSDs. To address
the degraded index quality under continuous updates, SPFresh in-
troduces a local rebuild mechanism that splits or merges partitions
and reassigns vectors to their nearest partitions.
However, when extending SSD-based ANNS to broader large-
scale scenarios, their update performance becomes a limitation.
Recent industrial practice points out that, to track user behavior
in real time, the continual learning [22, 38, 42, 69] of embedding
models calls for high-throughput updates. For a 0.1B-scale index in
recommendation systems, maintaining freshness demands an up-
date throughput of up to 30K queries per second (QPS) to match [29].
This requirement is comparable to the search throughput of online
services, making updates the other first-class performance target.
But even if we deploy an SSD array with >100GB/s bandwidth and
exhaust all 64 CPU cores on update-only operations, OdinANN and
SPFresh can only sustain about 6% to 40% of the target.
Graph-based OdinANN achieves only 2–3 KQPS because each up-
date searches ∼100 neighbors before modifying links, incurring ∼4–
7 ms due to inherent multi-hop SSD accesses [29, 30, 62]. Partition-
based SPFresh routes inserts through in-memory centroids (<1 ms),
but rewriting SSD posting lists per insertion in foreground still
incurs unacceptable CPU consumptions. Background per-vector
relocations after splits or merges require extra CPU cores to deal
with centroid locations and partition updates. Even with 64 CPU
cores for update-only requests, it can only reach ∼12 KQPS.
Hence, we propose to use buffered updates and batched reclus-
tering for rebuilds, instead of immediate flushes and asynchronous
reassignments in SPFresh. First, buffered updates can amortize the
overhead of foreground SSD round trips, where multiple updates to
the same cluster are accumulated in memory and flushed to SSD as
a batch. Second, we can recluster an imbalanced region, replacing
the steady drip of per-vector reassignments with a single batch
local rebuild. However, it is non-trivial to apply these ideas. There
are several conflicts and challenges.

Conflicts. For high-performance search, SPFresh takes fine-
grained partitioning and replicated placement. However, these de-
signs leave no memory budget for per-list buffers, and make the
buffering less effective. SPFresh creates up to 16% as many parti-
tions as vectors, so even small per-partition buffers (e.g., 8 or 16
vectors) can raise memory footprint to 2–3×. And a single new
vector can trigger up to 8× insertions, which again cause frequent
SSD reads/writes even with buffered updates.
Challenges. Hence, we return to normal-scale partitions (e.g., 1-
2% of the dataset size) and single-copy placement, but this burdens
search. In detail, it can suffer more from distribution drift, since
updates are amortized across fewer partitions, each partition expe-
riences more modifications, thus reducing the representativeness
of static centroids. Without replications, more clusters need to be
searched for each query simultaneously to keep accuracy. To ad-
dress these, we need dynamic centroid adjustment to track updates,
high concurrency and better pipelines to support search perfor-
mance. Moreover, we use reclustering to replace reassignments
after splits or merges, but reclustering incurs a higher computa-
tional burden and requires equal or more cores than background
reassignments, making reclustering inefficient.
In this paper, we propose PrairieFire with the following key
techniques:
• Based on above analysis, PrairieFire organizes a three-level
partition-based index and supports cascaded updates on cen-
troids, where insertions and deletions of vectors can be buffered
and propagated to upper-level centroids, thus avoiding frequent
SSD round trips and preserving centroid representativeness.
• Based on the index structure, we develop fine-grained concur-
rency control to support high concurrency of operations, includ-
ing insertions, deletions, searches and local rebuilds.
• We build the co-designed workflow on GPUs, CPUs and high-
bandwidth SSDs to accelerate centroid routing, partition scan-
ning, and computing-intensive clustering-based rebuilds.
We compare PrairieFire with state-of-the-art ANNS that sup-
port updates on large-scale workloads. Comprehensive evaluations
show that PrairieFire can sustain ∼3-35× update throughput while
maintaining ∼3-5× search throughput simultaneously. In addition,
during the whole process, our search accuracy is comparable to or
higher than baselines. At last, we evaluate the cost and energy effi-
ciency of PrairieFire, showing up to 1.5-8.7× hardware efficiency
of baselines in terms of device acquisition, power consumption, and
storage footprint. PrairieFire has been running reliably in Red-
Note’s services for two months, reducing index rebuild frequency
by over 95% across the dozens of indexes it hosts and improving all
business metrics (e.g., click-through rate and content impressions).

BACKGROUND
2.1
Freshness Challenges of Large-Scale ANNS
ANNS has become a fundamental infrastructure component [20,
21, 27] for large-scale search, recommendation, and advertising
services over embedding collections that often contain billions of
vectors. To reduce CapEx and OpEx at this scale, recent ANNS sys-
tems increasingly rely on SSD-based deployments [17, 23, 57, 62].
Since modern SSD arrays can provide sufficient bandwidth ap-
proaching that of DRAM [26, 37], we have deployed SSD-based
Helmsman [29] at scale for online services in RedNote (a.k.a. Xi-
aohongshu) [3]. Under strict 5-10ms search latency service level
agreement (SLA), it only consumes dozens of multi-SSD servers
to handle vector search services that previously required clusters
equipped with tens of thousands of CPU cores and PB-level DRAM.
However, if it can only support search on static collections, using
SSD-based storage [29] to offload memory pressure alone would
be insufficient. In practice, vector collections are highly dynamic
and require continuous updates. Changes to products, articles, and
embeddings must be reflected promptly to avoid stale or invalid
results [28, 55, 61]. In the search services, daily full refreshes are
common to keep the index up-to-date. Moreover, recommenda-
tion systems frequently retrain and deploy embedding models at
hourly or finer intervals to capture evolving user behavior [38, 42].
These model changes can modify vector representations, requiring
corresponding index updates [22, 69].
2.2
Operations for Dynamic Workloads
To make the dynamic vector changes timely visible in these services,
three operations [24, 51, 64, 67] are required:
• Search (Top-𝑘, Vector) takes a query vector as input and re-
turns the top-𝑘approximate nearest neighbors from the current
valid collection. In dynamic ANNS, search should be performed
over the real-time state of the collection, reflecting preceding
insertions and deletions.
• Insert (ID, Vector) inserts a new vector into the collection, where
ID denotes the unique identifier of the vector and Vector denotes
its embedding representation. After insertion, the new vector
should be included to subsequent searches and potential results.
• Delete (ID) removes an existing vector from the collection ac-
cording to its ID. After deletion, the removed vector would no
longer appear in subsequent search results.
2.3
Existing Solutions
Graph-based solution. Recent works [24, 55, 67] demonstrate the
capability to support updatable ANNS by online maintaining the
SSD-resident graph structure [57].
Specifically, FreshDiskANN and Greator [55, 67] buffer newly
inserted vectors in an in-memory graph and periodically merge
them in batches into the global on-disk graph index. However,
this mechanism can incur unacceptable memory overhead and
suboptimal search performance during graph merging (e.g., 32-
GB peak memory usage and 1.5-2× search latency when merging
6M vectors on 60-GB index of 100M vectors). To address these
limitations, OdinANN [24] adopts direct insertion, which searches
neighboring nodes for each insertion, updates related edges, creates
a new node, and directly flushes the changes to the NVMe SSD. By
avoiding periodic merging and optimizing immediate maintenance,
OdinANN provides higher and more stable performance of updates
and search with lower memory usage.
Partition-based solution. Following the partition-based struc-
ture of SPANN [17], SPFresh [64] stores partition-level metadata
in memory (e.g., centroid index, concurrency locks, and cluster
sizes) and stores vector partitions as posting lists on the NVMe
SSD. It supports streaming updates with a lightweight incremental
rebalancing protocol, namely LIRE.

MOTIVATION
3.1
Insufficient Performance
Since items and embedding models are continuously generated
and modified to improve user experience and revenue-related met-
rics [38, 39], we require real-time index updates to ensure embed-
ding freshness. To replace periodic full-index rebuilding, online
updates must sustain the required refresh rate, estimated as the
index cardinality divided by the full-refresh interval [29]. Represen-
tative workloads [42, 58], rebuilding a billion-vector index every 10
hours or a 100-million-vector index every hour, thus require ∼30K
QPS of updates (1 insertion and 1 deletion per update).
However, existing state-of-the-art solutions fall short of
production update demands. Even under optimistic update-only
workloads with concurrent searches disabled, state-of-the-art sys-
tems OdinANN and SPFresh fall far short of this target. As shown
in Figure 1a, even when dedicating all threads to updates on up to
64 CPU cores, they yield only 2–12K QPS in a single-server setup,
at most 40% of the required 30K QPS.
OdinANN is bottlenecked by its insertion path. During its inser-
tion, each new vector must first search the SSD-resident graph to
identify the top-100 (i.e., the number of neighbors to be connected
by new edges) nearest existing nodes, after which OdinANN ac-
quires exclusive locks on these nodes to apply the update. These
multi-hop SSD accesses from searching existing neighbors intro-
duce most of the overhead (i.e., 4–7 ms, up to 80% of the total
time), while lock contention from nodes’ updates further limits
concurrency of this graph-based structure.
Partition-based SPFresh delivers better update throughput than
graph-based OdinANN because it only searches an in-memory cen-
troid index to locate several nearest clusters for insertion, which is
faster (i.e., <1 ms) than OdinANN’s on-SSD top-100 graph search. It
then locks these clusters and updates their SSD pages with batched
I/O. However, it still remains ∼60% below the required throughput
when all threads are used for updates.

3.2
Does Bandwidth Scaling Work?
Scaling SSD bandwidth fails. To break the bandwidth bottleneck
of partition-based solutions, we have deployed multiple SSDs to im-
prove search throughput in industrial services [29], which inspires
us to explore whether updates benefit similarly from this increased
I/O bandwidth of modern NVMe SSD arrays [29, 30, 32, 63].
As shown in Figure 1b, results are disappointing. First, higher
bandwidth provides little benefit to OdinANN, whose performance
is dominated by SSD latency rather than bandwidth, due to its
multi-hop graph-based access pattern [32]. Then, we might expect
SPFresh to benefit more from increased I/O bandwidth, given its
bandwidth-friendly batched I/O pattern and partition-based index
structure [29, 64]. Unfortunately, SPFresh also improves hardly in
update performance at all, far from the near-linear scaling often
observed in the pure search-only workloads [29]. Even with 10
Gen5 NVMe SSDs providing almost an order of magnitude more
available bandwidth (i.e., ∼120 GB/s), update throughput improves
by less than 10%.
To understand the reason, we analyze the bandwidth utilization
and the achievable throughput as scaling the update concurrency
by increasing cores, as shown in Figure 2.
For SPFresh updates, bandwidth is not the bottleneck, but
the high CPU core consumption is. First, we measure the actual
bandwidth usage of 10 PCIe-Gen5 SSDs for SPFresh as we increase
update concurrency. As shown in Figure 2a, even with 10 SSDs
providing ∼120 GB/s of bandwidth, the bandwidth usage remains
below 1 GB/s, indicating that the available bandwidth is not the
bottleneck. Hence, we further estimate how many CPU cores for
concurrent updates would be required to achieve the target.
Unfortunately, as shown in Figure 2b, though partition-based
SPFresh already reduces modification-selection time (i.e., only locat-
ing the relevant clusters in memory but not searching neighbors on
SSDs as OdinANN), it still requires 128–256 cores theoretically for
the target throughput even without reserving CPU cores for search
requests, which is far beyond the available hardware resources in
real-world industrial deployments.
3.3
Where Does CPU Cost Come From?
To address the impractical consumption of CPU cores required for
the target update throughput, we first look back at the update pro-
cedures in SPFresh, and then analyze where the CPU consumption
comes from.

Procedure of updates. In SPFresh, update threads consist of
both foreground threads and background threads. As shown in Fig-
ure 3, SPFresh supports Insert and Delete operations in foreground
threads, and performs rebuild operations to maintain the index
quality in background threads.
For Insert, 1 it first locates relevant clusters for the new vector by
searching the in-memory centroid graph via node traversal. Then, it
updates these clusters’ SSD lists with read-modify-write round-trip
I/Os (i.e., 2 reads for loading and 3 following writes for flushing
modifications). Delete is lightweight because it only adds the version
value of the deleted vector’s ID in the in-memory version map.
Hence, most of the foreground update threads are allocated for
Insert (e.g., ∼40 cores, two thirds of the total in update-only stress
tests) and only 1 core is enough for Delete of SPFresh [64].
For rebuild, when the size of clusters exceeds a threshold (e.g.,
>128) or undersized (e.g., <10), the rebuilds are triggered to split
oversized clusters into two smaller ones or directly append small
clusters into a larger one. Then, to address the possible violation of
nearest partition assignments (NPA), it reassigns vectors from these
rebuilt clusters. In this process, it would first 1 read vector lists
of these clusters from SSDs, then 2 check whether vectors need
reassignment, relocate the nearest clusters for vectors potentially
violating NPA, and 3 finally write them back to SSDs. Due to the
expensive reassignments [64], the rebuilds are performed with the
other one third of the total cores in the background.
Consumption breakdown. Based on Perf [1], we analyze the
CPU cycles spent on foreground and background, as shown in Fig-
ure 4. In update-only stress tests with 4–64 threads, foreground
updates account for 59–62% of the total cycles, while background
rebuilds contribute the remaining 38–41%. Both paths repeatedly
perform per-vector centroid searches and posting-list updates by
traversing in-memory graph and reading/writing their lists on SSDs.
Specifically, centroid search by graph traversal accounts for around
30–35% of cycles, while round-trip read-modify-write updates dom-
inate the remaining cycles.
Foreground insertions trigger SSD reads and writes for every
new vector. As a result, updates generate frequent SSD accesses,
consuming huge CPU resources in I/O processing (e.g., submitting
commands, polling for completions) and clusters updating (e.g.,
modifying lists and metadata). And under continuous updates, clus-
ters can become imbalanced, triggering background rebuilds for
splitting or merging clusters to maintain the index quality. Then,
vectors can violate the NPA after splitting/merging imbalanced
clusters, necessitating background reassignments. The per-vector
reassignment of vectors further increases CPU cycle consumption
in background by triggering centroid searches and rewriting on-
SSD lists for the vectors affected.

OPPORTUNITIES AND CHALLENGES
4.1
Buffered Updates and Direct Rebuilds
Based on these observations, we identify two intuitive opportunities
for improving index maintenance efficiency and saving CPU cycles
to achieve higher update throughput.
Using buffered updates to amortize frequent round-trip
I/Os of per-vector read-modify-write operations in the fore-
ground threads. We can buffer new insertions and deletions in
memory for each cluster instead of being immediately written to
SSDs. Thus, by accumulating them, we can minimize every cluster’s
lists read-modify-write I/Os and flush them to the corresponding
SSD pages in a merged batch. As a result, foreground CPU cycles
spent on read/write operations from per-vector insertions can be
substantially reduced.
Using local reclustering-based rebuilds to avoid per-vector
relocation of reassignment in the background threads. When
an imbalanced cluster is split or merged, we can directly fetch its
nearby clusters and recluster them to generate new centroids and
posting lists. This preserves partition quality by reconstructing
neighboring clusters jointly, rather than repeatedly relocating indi-
vidual vectors [47, 48]. As a result, this generalized LIRE can replace
per-vector centroid searches and I/Os during relocations with a
single centroid search for the merged/split cluster’s centroid and
batched clusters refresh.
4.2
Conflicts and Challenges Arising
However, directly applying these ideas in SPFresh does not work
well. These two intuitive methods would conflict with current
search-oriented design choices and limited hardware capabilities.
We discuss three conflicts (Figure 5) and their corresponding chal-
lenges (Figure 6) that prevent buffering and reclustering from being
effective in practice, as follows.
Memory bloating. The first conflict arises from SPFresh’s fine-
grained partitioning and buffering. Following SPANN, SPFresh cre-
ates up to 16% as many clusters as the total number of vectors
to improve search efficiency [17]. However, maintaining per-list
buffers for merging each cluster’s SSD flushes further enlarges its
already high memory footprint. As shown in Figure 5a, SPFresh
(i.e., Origin) already consumes about 1.5× the raw dataset size. On
the ∼13GB SIFT100M dataset, increasing the per-list buffer count
from 2 to 16 adds extra 5-22 GB, and at the count of 16, the total
memory consumption approaches 3× the dataset size.
One straightforward solution is to reduce the number of clusters
to a normal IVF scale [5], making memory usage more affordable.
However, fewer clusters would absorb more member changes per

even with a buffer budget of 16 vectors per partition, the probability
of round-trip SSD access remains around 40%.
Therefore, to make buffering updates effective, we argue that
redundant placement can be removed. However, this shifts the cost
to search. As shown in Figure 6b, using single-thread 8-replica
search as the baseline, we compare search latency with and without
replicas under increasing search concurrency. Without redundancy,
search faces higher latency due to reading SSDs and computing
distances for more cluster lists; when concurrent search threads
reach 64 CPU cores, search latency further increases sharply due
to intensified hardware contention on concurrent CPU threads and
memory bandwidth of DRAM [29].
Computational burden. The third issue comes from comput-
ing burden of reclustering-based local rebuilds. While the batched
clustering avoids per-vector centroid searches and frequent up-
dates on-SSD posting lists for all vectors affected by imbalanced
partitions after splitting or merging, it introduces clustering that
has heavier computing consumption than centroid search during
background reassignments.

of foreground threads.
5
PRAIRIEFIRE
5.1
Considerations
Addressing these conflicts and trade-offs, to make buffered update
and batched rebuild practical, we have the following considerations.
First, we argue to use an index structure with normal
partitioning granularity, centroid-adaptive design, and
non-redundant placement. Normal-scale partitioning supports
buffered updates without an unacceptable memory footprint.
Centroid-adaptive design is for continuously tracking cluster
modifications to avoid degraded centroid representativeness.
Non-redundant placement eliminates the multiple insertion of the
same vector into different clusters, reducing SSD access frequency
and avoiding pseudo buffering.
Second, index operations call for an efficient concurrency con-
trol mechanism on this update-oriented index structure to pro-
ceed with minimal contention, enabling concurrent searches, up-
dates, and index maintenance.
Third, it is practical to introduce GPUs as extra computa-
tional resources along with efficient pipelines among various
hardware (i.e., GPUs, CPUs, and SSDs) to support heavier search
overhead from our update-oriented index structure, and to enable
direct clustering-based rebuilds without overwhelming CPUs.
5.2
Design Overview
Following these considerations, we propose PrairieFire. Figure 7
shows the design overview, including the index structure, concur-
rency control, and software-hardware co-design pipelines in the
update-search workflow.
Index structure ( §5.3). First, with normal-granularity partition-
ing for affordable buffering, PrairieFire organizes a three-level
partition-based index [5, 48] across SSDs, memory, and GPUs. From
bottom to top, it consists of vector partitions as level-2, partition
centroids as level-1, and routing centroids as level-0 (§5.3.1). Buffer-
ing updates and search operations are managed across these levels
simultaneously (§5.3.2), then local rebuild and cascaded centroid-
adaptation are applied to maintain index quality (§5.3.3).

Concurrency control ( §5.4). To support concurrent online
operations including searches, inserts, deletes, and background lo-
cal rebuilds, we design a granularity-decoupled locking scheme to
efficiently reduce concurrency contention. It is designed to narrow
the scope of locking by decoupling different levels of the hierar-
chical partition-based index. Then, we use the locking-validation-
execution pattern to ensure consistency, while preventing upper-
level locks from unnecessarily blocking operations on unrelated
lower-level objects in the index.
Software-hardware co-design ( §5.5). We enable a hetero-
geneous CPU-GPU-SSD architecture through software-hardware
co-design in PrairieFire. The array of NVMe SSDs provides high
I/O bandwidth, while GPUs offer strong parallelism to avoid CPU
computation burden during foreground search including level-0
routing centroids and level-2 vector partitions, and background
clustering-based local rebuilds.
5.3
Index Structure
PrairieFire organizes a bottom-up three-level clustering layout
across GPUs, host memory, and NVMe SSDs, supporting buffered
updates and cascaded adjustments.
5.3.1
Three-level layout. As shown in Figure 8, PrairieFire orga-
nizes a three-level layout, we describe each level from bottom to
top in detail below.
At Level-2, vectors are clustered into disjoint vector partitions
(VP), where each maintains its own in-memory buffer and on-SSD
continuous pages (e.g., 4 pages, 16 KB, can store 128 128-byte SIFT
vectors). Since clusters are independently updated and different
clusters’ pages are separated and non-contiguous on SSDs, we use
per-cluster buffers rather than a shared buffer [18, 55] to avoid
inter-cluster concurrency contention and keep buffered vectors
contiguous for cache-friendly access during search. IDs of both
buffered and on-SSD vectors are kept in host memory for fast
deletion (§5.3.2), but stored separately in two continuous arrays to
split buffered and on-SSD data so that IDs of buffered vectors can
be directly deleted and IDs of on-SSD vectors can be marked for
deletion with lazy garbage collection during flushes (§5.3.3).
At Level-1, partition centroids (PC) of level-2 vector partitions
are clustered in memory, where each level-1 entry (i.e., a partition
centroid) routes a level-2 vector partition.
At the top, Level-0 further consists of routing centroids (RC)
of clusters in level-1 partition centroids. PrairieFire stores a con-
tiguous host-side level-0 shadow with a one-to-one mapping to
GPU-resident routing centroids, whose slices are distributed across
GPUs for acceleration during level-0 search. Lazy synchroniza-
tion first applies cascaded level-0 centroid adjustments to the host

5.3.2
Operation paths. Based on the three-level layout described
above, Figure 9a further shows the foreground operation paths,
including search, insert, and delete.
Search first 1 searches level-0 on all flat GPU-resident slices to
get candidate partition centroid clusters, and 2 searches level-1 to
find several nearest candidate level-2 vector partitions. Finally, it
3 scans both buffered and on-SSD vectors of candidate level-2 VP
to get the final top-𝑘.
Insert also locates the target vector partition by searching level-
0 and level-1 ( 1 and 2 ), but only needs the nearest one for update.
After locating the target VP, PrairieFire 3 appends the new vector
to its buffer and records the vector’s ID in memory, and further
records the ID of the partition that receives this new vector in a
hash-based map.
Delete first A uses the map structure to find the vector partition
ID for the vector to be deleted. Then, PrairieFire B conducts the
deletion. If the vector is still in the buffer, it is removed by swap-last-
pop (i.e., swapping it with the last entry and popping) to keep the
layout contiguous. Otherwise, for an on-SSD vector, PrairieFire
only marks its ID as −1, filters it during search, and performs lazy
garbage collection during flushes, avoiding frequent SSD accesses
on the foreground deletion path as well.
5.3.3
Flush, local rebuild, and cascaded adjustment. Following the
above paths, foreground insert and delete can trigger flush and
rebuild operations when certain conditions are met, and cause
cascaded adjustments at higher levels.
In Figure 9b, case 1 shows the level-2 flush. When a buffer
becomes full (e.g., buffering 3 vectors reaches the budget in the
figure), or its on-SSD part contains too many invalid items (e.g., 3
IDs are −1), a flush is triggered. It merges buffered vectors and the
remaining valid on-SSD vectors, and writes them back to the same
SSD region as continuous pages. The buffer is then cleared and
the in-memory ID records are refreshed. Case 2 shows the back-
ground local rebuild, which is triggered when the vector partition
becomes imbalanced following SPFresh [64] (i.e., it is undersized
or oversized). In contrast, PrairieFire directly performs recluster-
ing on the imbalanced vector partition and its nearby partitions,
regenerating balanced partitions instead of SPFresh’s per-vector
conditional reassignments.

Across hierarchical levels, PrairieFire binds each centroid with

additional version record for consistency validation.
In addition, as mentioned in §5.3, PrairieFire maintains a parent
vector partition ID for each vector to support ID-based deletion and
remapping. To protect these ID mappings, but avoid expensive per-
vector locks, we use slot-based locks: each vector ID is hashed to a
lock slot by ID mod the count of slots. This reduces the lock memory
footprint from vector scale to slot scale (e.g., from 1B vector locks
to 0.1M slot locks), while preserving concurrency.

PrairieFire follows these three principles.
1. Record-level concurrency consistency. Designed for approx-
imate top-𝑘results [24, 64], PrairieFire ensures a record-level
immediate visibility [25, 61]. Once an insert or delete completes, its
effect is visible to subsequent searches: inserted vectors are included
for retrieval, and deleted vectors are excluded from results.
2. Decoupling with version validation. To avoid unnecessary
blocking (e.g., intra-cluster members should not block each other
just because they belong to the same parent cluster), PrairieFire
locks only units used in the current stage and releases them before
switching to the other granularity in the next stage. Since level-2
clusters may change between stages (e.g., some of the candidate
clusters returned by level-1 search might have been rebuilt), the
level-2 related stage validates recorded versions before access. On
mismatch, it can fall back to the previous stage or ignore invalid
items.
3. Ordered locks. To prevent deadlocks, operations that need
multiple locks acquire them in a deterministic order: higher-level
granularity before lower-level granularity (e.g., partition centroid
cluster before vector partition), and units at the same granularity
in ascending ID order (i.e., acquiring locks for clusters and slots
after sorting by IDs).

5.5
Software-Hardware Workflow
As shown in Figure 11a, to avoid the bandwidth bottleneck of host
memory [29] and support freshness requirements, PrairieFire runs
on a CPU-GPU-SSD architecture with high-bandwidth intercon-
nects that allow direct GPU-SSD I/O with ∼50 GB/s between each
GPU and 5 NVMe SSDs [9, 40, 53, 68]. This architecture provides
high theoretical throughput for concurrent search and update, but
also requires careful software-hardware co-design to avoid bot-
tlenecks and underutilization. We identify three key stages that
require customized optimizations: search level-0 on GPUs, search
level-2 across CPUs, GPUs, and SSDs, and rebuilds on GPUs.
5.5.1
Batched Distance Computation for Level-0. As shown in Fig-
ure 11b, a naive implementation launches a level-0 scan for each
search or insert independently. It performs GEMV distance compu-
tation [6] followed by GPU ranking (i.e., radix-based selection [8,
70]), over all level-0 vectors; both stages are bandwidth-bound. This
becomes severe at large scale: for 1B-scale SIFT [44], level-0 can
contain 105–2 × 105 128-dimensional FP16 vectors in experiments.
GEMV alone reads 24–50 MB per request, requiring 1.4-3 TB/s of
bandwidth at the target throughput (i.e., 60K aggregated from 30K
search and 30K update QPS [29]). Moreover, radix-style ranking
repeatedly reads and writes the distance/id array; four read-write
passes (32-bit distance / 8 bits per radix pass) add 6–13 MB per
request. These together can demand ∼1.8–3.8 TB/s that can ex-
haust the actual GPU memory bandwidth (e.g., two GPUs provide
2-2.6 TB/s in our platform as §6.1).
To address this issue, PrairieFire packs concurrent level-0 dis-
tance computing into continuous dynamic batched GPU opera-
tions [66] across all GPUs, which allows for better utilization of
GPU resources and reduces memory bandwidth pressure. As shown
in (B) of Figure 11b, on each GPU, a worker gathers queries from
search and insert workers, forms a dense query batch, and com-
putes query-RC (routing centroid) distances with GEMM. It then
scatters each query’s distance vector back to its foreground worker,
which performs GPU ranking to select candidate partition centroid
clusters. Packing isolated GEMV into batched GEMM reuses rout-
ing centroid reads across queries, improves arithmetic intensity,
and significantly reduces per-request bandwidth demand.

5.5.3
Fusing kernels for micro-scale clustering. As shown in Fig-
ure 12b, local rebuilds in PrairieFire are micro-scale: each rebuild
reclusters at most 64 nearby vector partitions, usually involving
thousands of vectors. At this scale, standard GPU KMeans back-
ends [13, 14] are inefficient. Their Lloyd iterations commonly in-
voke multiple GPU kernels, including GEMM-based distance com-
putation, sorting, assignment, and reduction for new centroids,
where kernel launches and intermediate global memory (i.e., G-
Mem) traffic can result in a rebuild time of up to 10-30 ms, reducing
GPU efficiency.
PrairieFire therefore designs a fused micro-scale clustering
kernel as shown in (B) of Figure 12b. Because the centroids are
small (e.g., 64×128-dimensional FP32 vectors take only 32 KB), they
can be cached in GPU shared memory (i.e., S-Mem, hundreds of KB
per streaming multiprocessor). We use a design in which each GPU
thread processes one vector, computes distances to all centroids,
finds the nearest cluster, and immediately updates the cluster’s
count and vector sum through atomic accumulation. These statistics
are then used to compute centroids for the next Lloyd iteration. By
fusing assignment with statistics accumulation, PrairieFire avoids
materializing and regrouping assignments, reduces global-memory
traffic, and removes several kernel launches from the CPU side,
achieving <5ms micro-scale clustering.

EVALUATION
We conduct evaluations for the following questions:
• How does PrairieFire compare with advanced SSD-based
systems by performance under standard workloads? (§6.2)
• What are the sensitive configurations of PrairieFire? And
how do PrairieFire’s techniques and extra hardware con-
tribute to its performance improvements? (§6.3)
• What is the hardware efficiency of PrairieFire in terms of
device price, energy, and storage? (§6.4)
• How does PrairieFire compare with GPU-based real-time
ANNS and perform under higher dimensional datasets and
production workloads? (§6.5)
6.1
Experimental Setup
Platform. For evaluations of run-time performance, we use the
same platform as our production services’ deployment settings,
which comes with an EPYC 64-core Genoa CPU, 12-channel DDR5
memory, 10× 2TB PCIe-Gen5 NVMe TLC SSDs, and 2 NVIDIA RTX
Pro 5000 Blackwell GPUs.
Workloads. Following previous work [24, 64], we use three widely
used standard dynamic workloads, unless otherwise stated.
• Workload A simulates the scenario, where all vectors are updated
(i.e., deleted and re-inserted with new embeddings) on a 100M
subset of SIFT1B [44]. It consists of 128-dimensional uint8 vec-
tors and its similarity is measured using Euclidean distance. We
extract two disjoint 100M vectors from SIFT1B, where one is
used as the initial dataset and the other is used for updates. Each
update is performed by deleting a vector from inserted vectors
and inserting a new vector from the update dataset.
• Workload B has the same setting as Workload A but with 100 mil-
lion vectors of SPACEV1B [7], which consists of 100-dimensional
int8 vectors. Different from the uniform distribution of SIFT,
SPACEV has a skewed distribution [64].
• Workload C scales up the experiment to 1 billion vectors, where
the initial dataset is 900M vectors in SIFT1B and the update
dataset is the remaining 100M vectors.
Comparisons. We compare PrairieFire with state-of-the-art
SSD-based ANNS systems that support updates: Greator [67],
OdinANN [24] and SPFresh [64]. The parameters are kept at
optimally default values recommended, except that Greator’s
and OdinANN’s graph-based beam width is set to 16 for better
bandwidth utilization on multi-SSD setups [29]. We do not include
FreshDiskANN [55] in comparisons, as it unacceptably incurs
prohibitive memory consumption–up to 5-6× the dataset size and
has been proven to perform worse (i.e., maximally 3K QPS updates
and 5K QPS searches) than others. Since SPFresh’s open-source
implementation does not support multi-SSD layout, we adapt it to
support multi-SSD operation using the same storage interface as
PrairieFire. Specifically, each posting list is stored in one device’s
contiguous pages and extended with a device identifier to route
SPDK [4] requests across SSDs. Graph-based SVFusion [52] is
included as the state-of-the-art GPU-CPU comparison.

6.2.1
0.1B-scale evaluation. As shown in Figure 13, we first eval-

the baselines in terms of throughputs of update (∼3-35×) and search

accuracy (i.e., recall@10), and the same level memory footprint.
For update, PrairieFire achieves ∼0.4 ms average latency per up-
date, because of buffered updates with merged I/Os. Hence, only 16
foreground threads are sufficient to achieve a high update through-
put. Benefiting from the optimized rebuilds (i.e., optimized GPU-
based batched reclustering), PrairieFire needs only 2 background
threads to match ∼40K QPS foreground updates. Greator suffers
from heavy merge operations on buffered and on-SSD graphs [24],
leading to the lowest update throughput. While avoiding heavy
merge operations, OdinANN still suffers from the high latency
and concurrency limitations of locks over the graph structure and
cannot achieve high-throughput updates. SPFresh achieves higher
update throughput than OdinANN, but still hits bottlenecks of
frequent on-SSD read-modify-write operations and heavy reassign-
ments, achieving only ∼10-12K QPS updates with 32 foreground
threads and 16 background threads. Consequently, few threads (i.e.,
16) are left for search, leading to low search throughput.
PrairieFire can allocate more threads (i.e., 46) to search, achiev-
ing much higher search concurrency. With 10 Gen5 NVMe SSDs
providing more than 100 GB/s of bandwidth and GPUs handling
computation, the I/O and host-memory-bandwidth bottlenecks in
the search path can be overcome. As a result, PrairieFire reaches
comparable recall by searching more clusters (e.g., ∼200) without
throughput loss. With normal-scale partitions (i.e., 1-2% of the
dataset size), PrairieFire can buffer up to 8 vectors per cluster,
while having a stable and acceptable memory usage. While Odi-
nANN’s memory can fluctuate due to merges on deletions, SPFresh
has a higher memory usage than ours due to more centroids. Since
Greator has unacceptably high memory usage (i.e., > 40 GB for the
0.1B scale and > 280 GB for the 1B scale) due to buffered graph and
global merge, it is omitted in figures of memory usage.

global memory traffic can be saved and both throughputs of updates
and searches still reach 36-39K QPS. Additionally, the recall is also
higher than baselines.

Storage-cost efficiency. Following recent work [29, 32], we evalu-

storage device with its unit cost. For multi-SSD SPFresh, each clus-

the SSD space for updates, leading to over 1.3 TB SSD and 85 GB
memory usage, costing about 2.9K$.
In contrast, though GPU memory is additionally introduced in
PrairieFire, only centroid-cluster centroids are stored on GPUs,
whose GPU memory footprint of the SIFT1B index is below 50 MB
(<$10 under Table 3). By reducing centroid counts, PrairieFire
also reduces SSD and host-memory usage to about 0.4 TB and
50 GB, respectively. The host memory mainly includes 14 GB update
buffers, 3-4 GB centroids, and others for metadata such as IDs,
parent mappings, and locks structure. As a result, the storage cost
of PrairieFire is ∼1.65K$. As shown in Figure 17c, this achieves
4.9–8.7× storage-cost efficiency of baselines.

GPU-based comparison, high-dimensional
datasets, and production evaluation
We compare PrairieFire with SVFusion [52], a state-of-the-art
graph-based real-time GPU ANNS, replicating its index to maxi-
mize multi-GPU performance. We use the optimal CPU–GPU mode
of SVFusion given its lack of I/O optimizations. Our system deliv-
ers approximately 2× higher update and search throughput over
Workload A as shown in Figure 18a, suggesting that partition-based
methods better suit concurrent update–search workloads on GPUs.
In addition to Workloads A and B of SIFT and SPACEV (i.e.,
ST-128 and SV-100), we evaluate 768-dimensional Wikipedia (i.e.,
WK-768) [10] and 960-dimensional Gist (i.e., GS-960) [49] against
OdinANN, the state-of-the-art graph-based system, as baselines in
update–search workloads. Though Figure 18b shows diminishing
gain of our system as dimensionality increases due to higher search
I/O than graph-based methods, PrairieFire maintains over ∼10×
update throughput and ∼1.5× search throughput.
We further evaluate PrairieFire over multiple epochs in one of
our production 128-dim recommendation workloads (Figure 18c),
where each epoch corresponds to an embedding model update and
a full index refresh. During the first refresh, clustering through
local rebuilds improves centroid quality, yielding 3-5% recall gains.
Then, recall would remain stable across subsequent epochs.

RELATED WORK
As real-world workloads now require ANNS systems to support bil-
lions of vectors [39, 54, 56], in-memory solutions [5, 16, 19, 46, 65]
are replaced by disk-based approaches. DiskANN [57] and Star-
ling [62] are representative examples following the graph-based
pattern. PipeANN [23] further optimizes the graph-based approach
by pipelining the search process. These graph-based systems have
higher throughput than cluster-based SPANN [17] on a single SSD,
because they consume less I/O bandwidth. However, as the array
of modern SSDs becomes more powerful, cluster-based approaches
can also achieve high throughput with low latency [17, 29, 30, 64],
becoming popular again. At the same time, freshness requirements
are also increasing [38, 42], FreshDiskANN, Greator, OdinANN,
SPFresh, and SVFusion [24, 52, 55, 64, 67] are proposed to sup-
port updatable ANNS in large-scale scenarios, but their perfor-
mance is limited under high update rates. Quake [48] is proposed
for CPU-only index maintenance and does not support concur-
rent update and search. GPU-based, FPGA-based, and CXL-based
ANNS [31, 32, 33, 34, 41, 50, 59, 63] are also proposed to address
the limitation of CPUs, but they do not support updates.
9
CONCLUSION
We argue that buffered update and cluster-based index are effective
for supporting updatable ANNS in large-scale scenarios. We pro-
pose PrairieFire, a CPU-GPU-SSD ANNS system that supports
high-throughput updates, preserving high-throughput search. Eval-
uations show that it can achieve the performance requirements of
high-freshness applications while maintaining high cost efficiency,
energy efficiency, and storage efficiency.

Abstract
The rapid proliferation of cloud computing and artificial intelligence workloads
is fundamentally reshaping the magnitude, spatial concentration, and electrical
characteristics of data center power demand. Data centers differ from conven-
tional commercial loads because they combine high rack densities, converter-
dominated interfaces, thermally coupled cooling, uninterruptible power supplies,
programmable workloads, and increasingly complex behind-the-meter generation
and storage within a single facility. This review examines their grid integration from
utility and sustainable-energy perspectives across resource planning, large-load
interconnection, distribution feeders, market operation, real-time dynamics, power
quality, and asset management. Particular attention is given to distribution-level
issues that receive limited treatment in broader energy reviews: bidirectional opera-
tion on renewable-rich feeders, harmonic propagation and resonance, ride-through
and reconnection, transformer derating, cable ampacity, neutral-conductor heating,
protection, and measurement. Static, composite, physics-based dynamic, calibrated,
impedance, electromagnetic transient, and hybrid load models are compared ac-

1. Introduction
Data centers support cloud services, enterprise computing, artificial intelligence
(AI), high-performance computing, and digital communication. Their electricity
use has become a planning and operating concern for power systems rather than
only an internal efficiency issue. Multiple assessments project substantial growth,
but their ranges differ because they depend on workload adoption, hardware and
cooling efficiency, utilization, project realization, and siting assumptions [1–5].
Earlier estimates provide useful historical baselines but should not be extrapolated
directly to the current AI-driven buildout [6, 7]. Recent North American Elec-
tric Reliability Corporation (NERC) reliability assessments and Federal Energy
Regulatory Commission (FERC) staff market reports treat large computational
loads as material considerations in resource adequacy, transmission planning, and
electricity markets [4, 8].
Annual energy use and requested peak demand do not, by themselves, describe
grid risk. Two facilities with the same peak megawatt rating may have materially
different effects. One may have controlled ramps, verified ride-through, timely
telemetry, and coordinated storage; another may have voltage-sensitive protection,
uncertain converter behavior, or a weak point of interconnection (POI). Data
centers are better represented as concentrated, converter-interfaced, thermally
coupled loads with high service-continuity requirements and partly programmable
demand than as a single static demand block [9–11].
The literature has developed along several partly disconnected lines. Facility re-
views address cooling, power usage effectiveness (PUE), internal energy modeling,
and integrated energy systems [12–14]. Flexibility and storage reviews examine
workload scheduling, uninterruptible power supply (UPS) systems, batteries, and
ancillary services [15–17]. Power-system surveys [18–20] and regional forecasts
[21] examine AI demand, planning, markets, and stability. Less synthesis connects
these topics to feeder behavior, measurement-driven load models, power quality,
equipment aging, and implementation of emerging reliability requirements.
The scope is the utility interface of large computational loads across grid layers
and time scales. This interface is not confined to the transmission system. Although
the largest hyperscale campuses typically connect at transmission voltage through
dedicated substations, a substantial share of near-term growth is expected to be
served from shared, mixed-use distribution systems, and distribution utilities are
consequently receiving a growing volume of large-load interconnection requests
that range from multi-megawatt edge facilities to campus expansions of tens of
megawatts [22, 23]. Studies of data centers report effects on feeder reliability

[24], phase balance [25], voltage control [26], and reconfiguration and hosting
capacity [27]. Adjacent work on power-electronic loading provides the transferable
transformer and asset mechanisms needed to interpret these local effects [28, 29].
This review develops an integrated grid-layer and time-scale taxonomy that
links facility characteristics to planning, interconnection, distribution operation,
markets, dynamics, power quality, and asset aging. It compares static, composite,
physics-based dynamic, calibrated, converter, electromagnetic transient (EMT),
and hybrid load models. It also connects harmonic-rich demand with transformer
derating [28, 30], cable ampacity [31], neutral-current accumulation [32], and
asset management using representative transformer, cable, and computer-load
evidence. NERC [23, 33] and FERC [8, 34] developments are interpreted as
a North American implementation case for reliability practice, computational-
load modeling, interconnection, and market design. Mitigation pathways are
organized around distributed energy resource management systems (DERMS)
and advanced distribution management systems (ADMS) [35], UPS and storage
control [36, 37], workload scheduling and demand-side measures [38], power-
quality design, asset monitoring, and supply co-location. Figures 1 and 2 define
the organizing framework for the remaining sections.
2. Review methodology, scope, and positioning
2.1. Scope, source selection, and evidence grading
This article is a problem-oriented critical review of the grid interface of data
centers and other large computational loads. The analysis spans facility operation,
resource and transmission planning, interconnection, distribution-system perfor-
mance, market participation, real-time dynamics, power quality, asset degradation,
and sustainable supply. The synthesis asks how data centers differ from conven-
tional large loads, how those differences propagate across grid layers and time
scales, which measurements and model classes are appropriate for each study ob-
jective, and which supply, control, and flexibility strategies can mitigate reliability
and sustainability risks.
Sources were assembled in three groups. Peer-reviewed journal and conference
papers were prioritized for technical mechanisms, model development, experi-
ments, and quantified results. Standards, national-laboratory, utility, and policy
reports, and official NERC and FERC records were used to describe current en-
gineering practice, reliability concerns, and regulatory implementation. Preprints
were included only where they provide the earliest available evidence on rapidly de-
veloping topics such as ride-through, reconnection, resonance, and calibrated data

measurement, which on simulation, and which on regulatory text.
2.2. Positioning relative to existing reviews
Prior reviews fall into four overlapping groups: energy-estimation studies [2, 3],
facility reviews [12–14], flexibility and storage reviews [15–17], and broader AI
and grid surveys [18, 19, 40]. The power-system surveys establish the importance
of demand growth, planning, markets, and stability, but a less developed connection
remains between those topics and feeder operation, distribution asset degradation,
measurement and calibration of computational-load models, power quality, and
implementation of reliability requirements [20]. This review therefore uses the
utility interface as its organizing unit, extending from large-load studies and hosting
capacity to ride-through, reconnection, converter dynamics, harmonic propagation,
equipment derating, and asset aging.
Official reliability and regulatory records provide implementation context
rather than substitutes for peer-reviewed technical studies. NERC records ad-
dress risk characterization, practice gaps, interconnection studies, commissioning,
and computational-load modeling [23, 33, 41], and FERC proceedings address
transmission interconnection, co-location, forecasting, and market implications

3. Data center operations and load characteristics
3.1. Load mix and facility coupling
Facility demand comprises information technology (IT) equipment, cooling,
power-conversion stages, UPS systems, backup-generation interfaces, lighting,
controls, and auxiliary services. Their shares vary with server utilization, cooling
technology, rack density, climate, and electrical architecture [12–14]. A universal
load-mix pie chart is therefore misleading. AI-oriented designs tend to increase
rack density, accelerator concentration, and the coupling between IT loading and
cooling demand, but the magnitude remains facility-specific [18, 46].
At the facility boundary, a compact accounting identity is
PDC(t) = PIT(t) + Pcool(t) + Ploss(t) + Paux(t),
(1)
where PDC is facility demand before explicit dispatch of on-site generation or
storage, PIT is productive computing power, Pcool is thermal-management demand,
Ploss includes transformer, UPS, power-distribution-unit, and conversion losses,
and Paux includes controls, security, fire protection, lighting, and other balance-of-
plant demand [13, 47, 48]. Eq. (1) is an accounting identity, not a dynamic load
model. Grid exchange requires separate signed terms for generation, charging, and
discharging.
Component shares are case-dependent. A dynamic model of a 10 MW facility
reports a prior estimate of approximately 60% IT energy use and 30% cooling
energy use. At the study’s selected peak operating point, its simulated server-
room and heating, ventilation, and air-conditioning components account for about
85% and 15% of facility power, respectively [48]. The contrast shows why fixed
pie charts can misstate both energy use and grid-facing dynamics. Illustrative
next-generation AI electrical-architecture roadmaps likewise describe technology-
specific scenarios rather than fleet-wide averages [46].

3.2. Workload stages and electrical variability
Computational demand is shaped by workload stage. Model preparation,
training, fine-tuning, and inference differ in duration, accelerator utilization, syn-
chronization, storage activity, and tolerance for delay. Training can sustain high
utilization yet exhibit periodic communication or checkpointing transients; infer-
ence follows user demand more closely and can be bursty; data preparation and
fine-tuning often occupy intermediate regimes [18, 50, 51]. These patterns are
not universal because model architecture, cluster size, scheduling, and hardware
change the electrical profile.
Hardware and software controls mediate the conversion from workload to
electrical demand. Power capping, accelerator scheduling, workload shaping,
cooling control, and rack-level storage can modify ramps and peaks [18, 51, 52].
Such controls create useful flexibility, but also make the load partly dependent on
internal policies that may be invisible to the utility unless models and telemetry are
exchanged.
For power-system analysis, flexibility is best separated into deferrable energy,
fast power modulation, and interruptible service. Deferrable energy moves nonur-
gent computation over hours or days and is bounded by deadlines and workload
placement [15, 53, 54]. Fast modulation changes power over seconds or minutes
without necessarily reducing total energy; storage-compute coordination and tie-
line smoothing studies illustrate this distinct service [52, 55, 56]. Interruptible or
flexible-interconnection service permits curtailment under specified conditions and
therefore requires explicit baselines, notice, recovery, and nonperformance terms
[34, 57].

3.3. Power electronics, UPS systems, cooling, and internal architecture
At the utility interface, a data center appears through transformers, switchgear,
rectifiers, power-factor-correction circuits, UPS systems, power distribution units,
server power supplies, cooling drives, and sometimes on-site storage or generation.
This chain differs from conventional motor-dominated demand because active and
reactive response depend on converter controls, current limits, protection, and
operating mode [10, 47, 58]. Depending on those controls, aggregate demand
can exhibit near-constant-power behavior over part of the voltage range and then
change sharply when current limits, transfer logic, or protection thresholds are
reached [10, 58].
UPS systems preserve critical computing service during supply disturbances
and may also provide grid support when controls, battery state, and service obli-
gations allow. Simulation, controller-hardware-in-the-loop, and demonstration
studies report potential for frequency response [52, 59], dynamic active-power
support and smoothing [36], line-interactive operation [37], and flexible-grid-asset
operation on a national-laboratory test platform [60]. These results establish tech-
nical potential rather than universal availability; backup reserve, duration, cycling,
and customer reliability remain binding constraints.
Cooling usually changes more slowly than compute demand because of ther-
mal inertia, while pumps, fans, chillers, and variable-speed drives add motor and
converter dynamics. Thermal storage and supervisory control can decouple part of
the cooling demand from short IT-load variations [14, 45], but cooling equipment
may also add harmonics and weather-sensitive peaks [61, 62]. Cooling dynamics
belong in the load model when the study concerns feeder loading, electromechani-
cal response, or power quality [12, 48]. Table 2 relates facility characteristics to
grid mechanisms.
4. Grid impacts across layers and time scales
4.1. Transmission planning and large-load interconnection
At bulk-system scale, difficulty arises from the size and pace of new demand.
Large-load records and utility assessments describe individual requests ranging
from tens to hundreds of megawatts, with some campus proposals larger still
[4, 23, 70]. Serving such projects may require staged energization, transmission
upgrades, generation procurement, and reliability studies [39, 57, 71]. When
several requests cluster geographically, uncertain project maturity and overlapping
in-service dates can distort forecasts and complicate sequencing [4, 39, 57].

4.2. Distribution-system and point of interconnection impacts
The distribution system is a growing, not a marginal, part of the large-load
problem. Many hyperscale campuses connect at transmission voltage through
12


dedicated substations, and a common assumption is that distribution utilities are
therefore bystanders. The opposite is increasingly the case: because transmission
headroom is limited and existing substations can often be expanded, a substantial
share of near-term growth is expected to receive service from shared, mixed-use
distribution systems, and distribution utilities are seeing a marked increase in
interconnection requests from data centers and other large loads. A 2026 national
assessment estimates that roughly 9–10 GW of the current United States project
pipeline could feasibly interconnect at distribution voltages, that requests now
range from edge facilities above 2 MW to campuses exceeding 100 MW, and
that meeting this demand could require on the order of 3000–4000 new feeders
and several thousand new substation transformers in the 13–34.5 kV classes [22].
Large campuses may therefore connect through transmission, subtransmission, or
dedicated distribution-like facilities at a bulk-system interface, while smaller and
midsize sites, campus distribution networks, and downstream auxiliaries remain
within utility distribution scope [22, 23]. Distribution studies show that data center
demand and flexibility may alter feeder reliability [24], phase balance [25], voltage
profiles [26], and post-fault reconfiguration and hosting capacity [27].
Distribution effects require a different evidence base from transmission plan-
ning. Assets are more numerous, observability is often lower, and equipment
is electrically closer to other customers. Direct hyperscale measurements at dis-
tribution points of interconnection remain scarce. Transformer studies establish
harmonic-heating and derating mechanisms [28, 30, 77]; cable studies establish
frequency-dependent ampacity and thermal constraints [31, 78, 79]; and computer-
load studies establish triplen-harmonic and aggregation effects in neutral conductors
[32, 80, 81]. These sources establish physical pathways, not universal magnitudes
for every facility. Static planning models may also miss converter controls and
disturbance behavior [20, 82].
Distributed energy resources (DERs) change the feeder problem from one-
way loading to time-varying net exchange. High photovoltaic penetration can
cause voltage rise, variability, and reverse power flow that require coordinated
voltage control [83]. Where export is permitted, a campus with photovoltaic or
wind generation and storage may import heavily during compute peaks and export
during maintenance, low utilization, or high renewable output [14, 84]. Import
and export envelopes depend on coincidence, storage state, export limits, feeder
impedance, and utility and behind-the-meter controls. Co-location therefore still
requires analysis of reverse flow, regulator duty, contingencies, and, where relevant,
harmonic and protection conditions.
Hierarchical voltage control, active-network management, and DERMS or
13


ADMS coordination can increase usable hosting capacity or enforce export limits,
although results remain feeder- and scenario-specific [35, 83, 85]. Data center
demand may absorb local renewable production in some hours and worsen peak
loading in others. Joint planning should consider facility demand, renewable
output, storage, voltage control, and network reinforcement together rather than
evaluate load and supply portfolios independently [14, 25, 84]. Effective operation
also depends on observability, communications, model fidelity, and enforceable
operating rules [86, 87].
4.3. Markets, tariffs, and operational flexibility
Data center demand affects wholesale and retail markets through energy use,
coincident peaks, capacity obligations, congestion, and reserve requirements. Work-
load scheduling and geographic shifting can reduce cost or congestion under spec-
ified assumptions [15, 53, 54]. Storage-compute coordination can reduce peaks
or tie-line variability [55, 56, 59]. Emissions outcomes remain conditional on the
marginal generation mix and the time and destination of shifted demand [88, 89].
Service-level agreements, latency, workload value, cooling, and recovery constrain
the available response.
Market design should ask which service a facility can provide, with what
notice, duration, verification, and opportunity cost. The relevant products include
load shifting, power capping, storage response, interruptible service, and flexible
interconnection, not a binary classification of the site as flexible or inflexible.
Technical studies [54, 90] and policy records [34, 43, 57] increasingly pair these
products with improved forecasting, explicit tariff provisions, and performance
obligations.
Aggregation creates both value and common-mode risk. A common operator
can coordinate geographically dispersed facilities, but shared scheduling, firmware,
or protection logic may also synchronize recovery, disconnection, or reconnection.
These outcomes are plausible coordination risks rather than universally observed be-
havior. Programs should specify telemetry, recovery ramps, rebound management,
and performance verification [11, 15, 56].
4.4. Real-time dynamics, ride-through, and stability
On sub-second to minute time scales, data centers interact with voltage and
frequency through converter controls, UPS transfers, protection, load tripping, and
workload modulation. Facility-level dynamic models represent aggregate UPS,
cooling, converter, and workload behavior [10, 48, 73]. Impedance and resonance
studies address frequency-dependent converter-network interactions [64, 91, 92].
14


EMT and ride-through models are needed when switching, protection, current
limiting, or reconnection transients can change the conclusion [67, 69, 74].
System risk depends on how the facility behaves during and after a disturbance:
whether it stays connected, how active and reactive power change, whether internal
blocks transfer or trip together, and how quickly demand returns. NERC guidance
and alerts identify modeling, instrumentation, commissioning, protection, and
control as immediate areas for computational loads [33, 41, 72]. Technical studies
address the same issues through ride-through, reconnection, and stability analysis
[67, 68, 93].
Ride-through is not a binary attribute. Relevant parameters include voltage
and frequency thresholds, delay, active and reactive response, recovery shape,
internal segmentation, backup generation, UPS energy state, and reconnection
ramp. Studies of low-voltage ride-through [67], safe reconnection [68], large
power electronic loads [93], and fast frequency response [52] show that facility
controls and grid requirements must be designed together. Table 3 summarizes the
principal impact categories by grid layer and time scale.

5.2. Transformer heating, derating, and insulation aging
The transformer literature combines studies of increased power-electronic
penetration with broader nonlinear-load research. The former identify additional
losses and thermal duty and motivate condition-informed management [28, 29].
The broader literature shows that sustained loading raises top-oil and winding
temperatures, while harmonic current increases winding-eddy and other stray
losses [30, 77]; the resulting hot-spot temperature affects insulation-aging rate
[100, 101]. Most thermal and derating studies are not field measurements from
hyperscale service transformers. They establish transferable mechanisms whose
magnitude must be determined from facility-specific spectra, loading, construction,
and cooling.

5.3. Cable ampacity, underground thermal constraints, and neutral conductors
Low-voltage cable studies show that nonsinusoidal current can increase phase-
conductor and neutral losses, with sensitivity to conductor size, installation method,
phase allocation, and zero-sequence content [31, 102, 103]. Underground medium-
voltage studies add sheath loss, burial arrangement, soil thermal resistivity, mutual
heating, and load-cycle effects [78, 79]. These effects are route- and design-specific;
they do not imply one universal harmonic derating factor.
Neutral conductors need separate assessment where many single-phase
computer-type supplies are present. Balanced fundamental phase currents do
not guarantee low neutral current because triplen harmonics are zero-sequence
quantities. Surveys and aggregation studies report both high neutral currents
and substantial sensitivity to device diversity, phase angles, and attenuation
[32, 80, 81]. Design should use measured or equipment-derived spectra and actual
phase allocation rather than assume complete addition or cancellation.
Route-specific thermal studies show that underground circuits can become a
limiting element under long duct banks, adverse soil conditions, multiple loaded
circuits, or significant sheath loss [78, 79], and that zero-sequence harmonic current
further erodes the margin [102, 103]. Applying that result to a data center campus
is a planning inference rather than a universal field observation. Ampacity studies
should therefore include the complete route, neutral or sheath paths, installation
environment, load cycle, and expected harmonic duty.
5.4. Protection, metering, and monitoring implications
Direct field documentation of data center-induced protection misoperation re-
mains limited. Rectifier and adjustable-speed-drive studies document distorted
current and associated equipment or measurement effects [104]. Reviews of
converter-dominated protection identify control-dependent fault-current magnitude,
sequence content, and duration [105, 106]. These are engineering analogies, not
proof that a given data center will produce the same relay response. NERC’s
computational-load alert is more direct in calling for study, instrumentation, com-
missioning, protection, and control actions for large computational loads [33].
Risk-based monitoring can narrow this uncertainty. Depending on facility size,
voltage level, and network strength, useful records include synchronized active
and reactive power, voltage and current waveforms, harmonic spectra, event and
protection logs, UPS state, transformer thermal estimates, feeder topology, and
commissioning results [33, 95, 107]. The required package is study-specific; not
every installation needs every measurement at the same resolution.

large-load guidance concentrates on capacity, modeling, ride-through, commission-

5.5. Harmonic-constrained optimal power flow for converter-dominated loads
Conventional optimal power flow (OPF) formulations regulate fundamental-
frequency quantities such as active and reactive power, voltage magnitude, and
thermal loading, without representing the waveform distortion produced by non-
linear and converter-interfaced equipment. In networks with substantial harmonic
injection, a dispatch that is feasible at the fundamental frequency may still vi-
olate voltage or current distortion limits, increase transformer and cable losses,
disturb shunt capacitor banks or excite filter resonances, and shorten equipment
life. Harmonic-constrained optimal power flow (H-OPF) therefore co-optimizes
operating set points while enforcing harmonic power quality requirements such as
those of IEEE 519-2022 [97], using harmonic power-flow representations of the
kind described in IEEE 3002.8-2018 [108]. Formulations that embed IEEE 519
distortion limits in planning and dispatch problems have been developed mainly
for inverter-based distributed generation, where they determine the permissible
penetration, location, and size of converter-interfaced sources under harmonic and
protection constraints [109, 110].
This framing is directly relevant to large data centers, and increasingly to
AI-oriented facilities. Although accelerators operate internally from regulated
direct-current supplies, their aggregate demand is served through extensive power-
electronic infrastructure: high-capacity UPS rectifier and inverter stages, active
power-factor-correction (PFC) converters in server power supplies, direct-current
conversion stages supplying accelerator racks, battery-storage interfaces, and
variable-frequency drives (VFDs) for chillers, pumps, cooling towers, and air-
handling equipment [47, 58]. The simultaneous operation and rapid loading
variation of these devices can alter harmonic injections and create frequency-
dependent interactions with transformers, cables, capacitor banks, and harmonic
filters [64, 92]. The concern is amplified by the rapid expansion of data-center
electricity demand associated with AI workloads [1, 5].

6. Modeling requirements for data center loads
Figure 3 organizes model classes by the question they can answer. More
detail is not automatically better. The appropriate fidelity follows from the time
scale, network strength, protection behavior, data availability, and decision being
supported.
6.1. What static models can and cannot represent
Constant-power, constant-current, and constant-impedance models, and their
combination as the constant impedance-current-power (ZIP) model, remain useful
for fundamental-frequency power-flow screening and scenario analysis. A conven-
tional static voltage-dependence curve, however, cannot represent discrete UPS
states, converter current limits, protection thresholds, reconnection logic, harmonic
emission, storage state, or workload pulses [20, 33, 82]. This is a limitation of the
model class, not a claim that every facility exhibits every behavior.
Static models can identify steady-state thermal and voltage constraints, but
they cannot determine whether a facility rides through a sag, transfers to storage,
trips in blocks, excites a mode, or reconnects with an excessive ramp. Dynamic
models of data centers address aggregated operating states [10, 73]; impedance and
resonance models address modal interactions [92]; and EMT or transient-stability
models address fast ride-through and reconnection behavior [67–69]. Facilities
with the same net demand may need different dynamic representations because
UPS topology, protection, converter controls, cooling composition, and internal
segmentation differ [37, 82, 114].
6.2. Dynamic, calibrated, impedance, EMT, and hybrid representations
Physics-based aggregate models represent selected internal states such as UPS
or storage response, cooling motors, electronic load fractions, and pulsed com-
puting demand. They support phasor-domain stability and ride-through studies
when parameters are available, but aggregation may hide control interactions and
protection boundaries [48, 69, 74]. Calibrated or pattern-consistent models identify
aggregate behavior from measurements and may reduce disclosure of internal
equipment. Their validity can deteriorate when hardware, workload, or control
settings change [73].
Converter and impedance models address compatibility between the input be-
havior of power supplies or UPS stages and network impedance. They are suited to
resonance and control-stability questions but require frequency-dependent parame-
ters and defensible aggregation [64, 65, 91]. They do not replace a facility-level
ride-through model because the two representations answer different questions.

6.3. Calibration, data exchange, and model governance
Model validity depends on commissioning data and continued maintenance.
Workload, equipment, protection, and control information may be commercially
or security sensitive, while utilities still need enough behavior to assess stability,
power quality, restoration, and operating limits. The literature supports aggregated
or privacy-preserving exchange and digital assurance [113, 115], combined with
standard templates, commissioning tests, and disturbance-based validation [33, 72,
107].
The minimum data set follows the study purpose. Resource-adequacy anal-
ysis needs credible project maturity, energization dates, and demand scenarios.
Power-flow and hosting-capacity studies need location, maximum and minimum
demand, reactive capability, and voltage sensitivity. Dynamic studies need active
and reactive response, protection and ride-through states, UPS transfer, segmen-
tation, recovery, and reconnection. Power quality and asset studies need spectra,
impedance, filters, loading history, and thermal parameters [33, 69, 74].
Governance is needed because a computational facility may change after its
interconnection study. New accelerators, power supplies, cooling systems, UPS
modes, protection, or control settings may invalidate an accepted model. NERC
guidance [87] and alerts [33, 72] call for validation, updated information, commis-
sioning, instrumentation, and operational coordination for emerging or computa-
tional loads. Table 5 summarizes the intended use and limitations of the principal

8.1. Interconnection reform and study data
A large-load application should separate five information classes. Project-
maturity data cover site control, commercial readiness, staged energization, and
forecast uncertainty. Steady-state data cover maximum and minimum demand,
ramps, reactive capability, and contingency service. Dynamic data cover voltage
and frequency sensitivity, ride-through, segmentation, UPS transfer, protection,
and reconnection. Power quality data cover spectra, filters, switching states, and
relevant impedance. Operational data cover telemetry, communications, commis-
sioning, update obligations, and committed flexibility [23, 33, 72].
Standard templates can reduce omissions and repeated study cycles without
imposing identical requirements on every facility. Study scope should follow size,
voltage level, network strength, equipment, controllability, and the consequence of
common-mode behavior. Firm, flexible, staged, and connect-and-manage service
[38, 54, 125] may accelerate energization only when curtailment rights, telemetry,
operating limits, upgrade triggers, and cost allocation are explicit [57, 76].
8.2. DERMS, ADMS, and utility coordination
Distributed energy resource management systems and advanced distribution
management systems offer an architecture for coordinating data center demand
with feeder limits, voltage control, storage, and local generation. Most supporting
studies are generic, simulation-based, or demonstration-oriented rather than evi-
dence of routine production control of hyperscale fleets [35, 85, 86]. Their present
contribution is therefore architectural and prospective.
Implementation requires an operating agreement that converts planning as-
sumptions into measurable obligations: available reduction or injection, response
time, duration, state constraints, communications, measurement and verification,
cybersecurity, and fallback behavior. Without this link, a planning study may credit
flexibility that the operating platform cannot deliver reliably [15, 33, 107]. Field
demonstrations should test the complete planning-to-operations chain.
8.3. UPS and storage across time scales
UPS energy storage is intrinsic to many facilities, while additional battery
energy storage systems (BESS), flywheels, supercapacitors, and hybrid systems
can extend the range of grid services [16, 17]. Studies report peak reduction and
energy shifting [59, 125, 126], fast frequency or active-power support [36, 52], and
grid-forming or line-interactive operation [37, 127].
28


Storage controls must preserve the facility’s primary reliability function. Re-
sponse capability depends on state of charge, reserved backup energy, power and
energy rating, converter limits, duration, thermal constraints, cycling cost, and
recharge strategy. Correlated recharge after an event may create a secondary ramp
if recovery is not coordinated [16, 37, 125]. Interconnection studies should state
whether storage support is mandatory, contracted, or merely technically possible.
8.4. Workload flexibility, tariffs, and geographic shifting
Data center flexibility comprises distinct products: temporal shifting of delay-
tolerant work [15, 53], geographic redistribution [54, 56], and short-term power
capping, firm curtailment, and storage-mediated response [55]. These products dif-
fer in response time, duration, energy recovery, baseline, service-level consequence,
and network effect.
Optimization studies demonstrate potential, not universal dispatchability. La-
tency, service agreements, sunk computing capital, workload value, cooling re-
covery, data locality, and customer commitments constrain response. Tariffs and
contracts should define availability, measurement, compensation, rebound, non-
performance, and recovery [8, 38, 43]. Geographic shifting also needs destination
headroom and network bandwidth; moving computation can transfer congestion or
emissions rather than remove them.
8.5. Co-location, renewable integration, and clean firm supply
Co-location may combine computational demand with photovoltaic or wind
generation, storage, cogeneration, fuel cells, geothermal, nuclear, or other firm
supply. For sustainable-energy assessment, physical proximity and annual energy
matching are insufficient. Each configuration should be compared with a credible
counterfactual using hourly and locational matching, congestion, renewable cur-
tailment, marginal emissions, backup and balancing obligations, network upgrades,
asset duty, water and land use, and lifecycle effects [14, 17, 128].
On-site photovoltaic or wind generation with BESS may reduce net imports and
improve resilience, but performance depends on resource quality, load coincidence,
storage duration, export limits, and feeder hosting capacity. A Dublin case study
co-optimizes grid imports, photovoltaic generation, wind, storage, and a prospec-
tive small modular reactor portfolio; its findings are conditional on the assumed
costs, market design, and project scale [128]. A Bangladesh photovoltaic-battery
microgrid study similarly illustrates a location-specific resilience pathway, not a
universal least-cost design [84].
29


Cogeneration or trigeneration can raise total fuel utilization when a useful heat
or cooling sink is available. Fuel-cell cogeneration may also provide firm on-site
electricity and useful heat [129]. Neither option is inherently low-carbon. Net
benefit depends on fuel source and upstream emissions, electrical and thermal
efficiency, local air quality, water use, capacity factor, heat-sink utilization, and
the emissions of displaced grid supply [14, 130]. Natural-gas cogeneration should
be reported as a conditional reliability and efficiency pathway, not as renewable
supply.
Waste-heat recovery and thermal storage can lower cooling demand or serve
an external heat use, but feasibility depends on source temperature, the distance
and timing of a heat sink, space, piping, and the energy required for recovery or
upgrading. Heat pumps, district-heating links, chilled-water storage, and under-
ground thermal storage illustrate different temperature and duration requirements
[14, 44, 45]. Avoided energy and system emissions are more informative than
recovered heat quantity alone.
Co-location also does not establish electrical independence. A facility may
continue to use transmission service, ancillary services, backup supply, and system
balancing, and may alter deliverability or costs for other customers. The FERC co-
location record identifies these issues in the United States, but outcomes depend on
network and jurisdiction [42, 122, 131]. Table 7 compares the principal supply and
flexibility pathways and the conditions under which their benefits can be claimed.
8.6. Power quality design and asset monitoring
Power quality mitigation should follow the measured or modeled emission
spectrum and network impedance. Source-side converter design and filtering
address harmonic emission [61, 63]. Phase allocation and neutral design address
zero-sequence current [32, 80, 81]. Transformer decisions require harmonic-loss
and derating analysis [30, 77], and cable decisions require ampacity analysis under
nonsinusoidal current [31, 98]. Resonance screening requires both source and
network impedance [64, 65]. IEEE 519-2022 provides PCC distortion goals but
does not select a facility-specific filter, transformer, conductor, or resonance remedy
[97]; those decisions depend on the measured spectrum, impedance, operating
mode, and asset design.
Commissioning and periodic reassessment matter because server power sup-
plies, rack density, cooling drives, UPS modes, filters, and firmware can change
after energization. Temperature estimation, harmonic trending, loading history,
and event-triggered waveform capture support asset decisions and model updates

10. Conclusion
Data center grid impacts are governed by more than annual electricity use
or requested peak demand. Siting, project maturity, coincidence with system
peaks, ramping, converter and UPS behavior, voltage sensitivity, ride-through,
recovery, harmonic spectra, and verified flexibility determine the consequences for
generation adequacy, transmission planning, distribution operation, and markets.
34


Interconnection studies should therefore be based on staged and updatable facility
data rather than a single static megawatt value.
Distribution-level mechanisms are technically credible but remain less well
documented in hyperscale field settings than bulk-system impacts, even though
a growing share of new large-load requests is arriving at distribution utilities.
Harmonic and sustained current can increase transformer and cable thermal duty,
triplen components can load neutral conductors, frequency-dependent impedance
can produce resonance, and control or protection states can shape disturbance
response. Case-specific measurement, commissioning, spectrum and impedance
analysis, thermal assessment, and model revalidation are more defensible than
universal derating or protection assumptions.
Sustainable integration requires a similarly conditional assessment. Data cen-
ters may absorb variable renewable output, shift computing, provide storage-
mediated response, or support clean supply development, but they may also add
peak demand, backup obligations, reverse power flow, rebound, water use, and
asset stress. Annual renewable-energy matching or physical co-location alone
does not establish a net system benefit. Credible claims should account for hourly
and locational supply, network deliverability, marginal emissions, lifecycle effects,
water and land impacts, shared-grid obligations, and the operating capability that
is actually measured or contractually available. Coordinated planning, validated
models, transparent tariffs, interoperable controls, and asset-health monitoring are
therefore central to the reliable and sustainable growth of data center infrastructure
in the grid.

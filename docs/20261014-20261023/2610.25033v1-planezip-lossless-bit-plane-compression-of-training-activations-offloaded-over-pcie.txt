PlaneZip: Lossless Bit-Plane Compression of
Training Activations Offloaded over PCIe
Zhufan Gu, Qizhe Tang, Yuxin Wang, Xun Zhou, Hui Feng
Abstract—Training under a device-memory budget relies on
tensor rematerialization: an evicted activation is either swapped
to host memory or dropped and recomputed. Because GPU com-
pute has outgrown PCIe bandwidth, cost-model planners such as
MegTaiChi, which charge a swap its full transfer time, almost
never swap, and swaps that fill idle link time carry only a small
fraction of the saved activations. Saved activations, however, are
homogeneously typed arrays with bit-level redundancy beyond
zeros, which lightweight bit-plane compressors can remove at line
rate. We present PlaneZip, which compresses swapped tensors
on the copy path and plans around the compression. Its codec
picks, for every 128-byte block, the smallest of zero-value, delta
bit-plane, bit-plane, and a new zero-masked bit-plane encoding.
A per-tensor predictor reuses the ratio of the previous iteration,
and the planner budgets link time in compressed transfer times.
On activations captured from five models trained on real data, the
codec compresses BF16 activations by 1.71x and FP32 activations
by 1.39x (geometric means), against 1.18x and 1.20x for zero-
value compression. In trace-driven simulation of a PCIe 3.0
consumer GPU and a PCIe 4.0 datacenter GPU at half the
activation memory, PlaneZip cuts the rematerialization overhead
by 47% and 31% relative to a bandwidth-aware planner without
compression, and by 73% and 54% relative to MegTaiChi’s cost
model, with an estimated engine area of 0.036 square millimeters.

I. INTRODUCTION
The activations that the forward pass saves for the backward
pass dominate the memory footprint of DNN training [1],
[2]. When they exceed the device memory, systems either
oversubscribe it through unified virtual memory (UVM),
which migrates pages on demand [3], [4], or rematerialize
the activations explicitly. A saved tensor is either swapped
to host memory and prefetched before its backward use [5]–
[7], or dropped and recomputed from its inputs [1], [8], [9].
MegTaiChi [10] coordinates the two: when memory runs out,
it evicts the tensor with the lowest cost, charging a swap its
transfer time and a drop its recomputation time.
The two mechanisms scale differently. Recomputation runs
at GPU speed, and the two GPUs we model differ by 23×
in peak math throughput, from a Turing-class consumer card
training in FP32 to an Ampere-class datacenter card training
in BF16, while their host links differ by only 2× (PCIe 3.0
and 4.0). For the median saved byte, a swap takes 10–13×
longer than recomputing the same tensor (Section II-C), so a
per-tensor cost model almost never picks it: in our simulations
MegTaiChi’s policy reduces to pure recomputation in 58 of 70
configurations. Swapping still pays when it uses link time that
would otherwise idle, which bandwidth-aware planners such
as Capuchin [7] exploit. That idle time is short, though: during
one iteration the link can move only 6–37% of the saved bytes
of the five models we study.
Compression enlarges that budget. Saved activations are
large arrays of a single type, the property that Bit-Plane
Compression (BPC) [11] exploits in GPU memory traffic.
They contain zeros produced by ReLU and by causal attention
masks, and neighboring floating-point values tend to share
their high-order exponent bits. Hardware block compressors
designed for GPU and main memory [11], [12] encode a 128-
byte block in a few cycles, fast enough to sit on the copy path.
The compressing DMA engine cDMA [13] applied zero-value
compression (ZVC) to swapped activations, which captures
ReLU sparsity but nothing else, and its swap schedule did not
change with the compression it achieved.
This paper presents PlaneZip, which compresses swapped
tensors on the copy path and plans around the compression.
It rests on three observations from activations that we capture
in real training iterations:
• Activations compress beyond their zeros. A per-block
choice among four lightweight encodings, including a
zero-masked bit-plane encoding that we add, compresses
BF16 activations by 1.71× (geometric mean over five
models), against 1.18× for ZVC (Section V-A).
• Compressibility is a stable per-tensor property. Train-
ing repeats the same operator sequence every iteration,
which MegTaiChi exploits for its memory plans. Pre-
dicting each tensor’s compressed size from the previous
iteration errs by less than 1% of the transferred bytes
(Section V-B).
• Only a compression-aware planner turns compres-
sion into speed. Compressing the swaps chosen by
a compression-unaware planner shortens transfers but
leaves the freed link time unused. Budgeting the link
with compressed transfer times, and ranking tensors by
the recomputation or memory they save per second of
transfer, recovers it (Section V-D).

II. BACKGROUND AND MOTIVATION
A. Tensor Rematerialization
A training iteration runs a forward pass that saves activa-
tions, a backward pass that consumes them in reverse order,
and an optimizer step. A saved tensor t of mt bytes is idle
between its last forward use and its first backward use, and
the peak memory occurs near the boundary of the two passes,
when nearly every saved tensor is alive. Rematerialization
frees t during its idle interval in one of two ways:
• Swap. Copy t to pinned host memory once it is final,
free it when the copy completes, and prefetch it before
its first backward use [5], [6]. A swap costs nothing if
both copies overlap with computation, and stalls the GPU
otherwise.
• Drop. Free t after its last forward use and replay the
operators that produced it before its backward use [1],
[8]. The replay costs Cr(t), which grows if the inputs of
those operators were dropped as well [9].
MegTaiChi’s dynamic tensor evicting [10] decides between
the two when memory runs out. It evicts the resident tensor
that minimizes

[14].
B. Memory Compression
Hardware compressors for caches and memory operate on
small blocks and must decode in a few cycles [15]. BPC [11]
targets the homogeneously typed arrays that dominate GPU
memory. Its Delta-BitPlane-XOR (DBX) transform subtracts
neighboring 32-bit words of a 128-byte block, transposes the
deltas into bit-planes, and XORs adjacent planes. Small deltas
leave long runs of all-zero planes, which a simple prefix code
compresses. Compresso [12] adapted BPC to compress main
memory transparently to the operating system. It found that
the DBX transform is not always beneficial and compresses
each block with and without it, and it predicts incompressible
pages to avoid the data movement that compression otherwise
causes.

Swapping turns the GPU and the host into a two-tier
memory: a small fast tier and a large tier behind a narrow link.
Heterogeneous memory systems, from hybrid main memory to
CPU-FPGA SoCs [16], place data across such tiers, sometimes
compressing the fast tier to raise its capacity [17]. Yokoyama et
al. [18] found that splitting a PC-indexed reuse predictor [19]
by access type (vector or scalar) improves its accuracy at
equal capacity. We apply the same idea to compressibility:
a signature that identifies each tensor by the operator that
produced it predicts its compressed size (Section III-B).
C. Motivation
Figure 1 quantifies the gap with the traces and platforms
of Section IV. Figure 1a plots, for every saved tensor of the
five models, the ratio between its transfer time Cs and the
time to recompute it with its inputs resident. On the PCIe 3.0
consumer platform (A), 95% of the saved bytes cost more to
swap than to recompute, and the median ratio is 10.1×; on
the PCIe 4.0 datacenter platform (B), over 99.9% do, and the
median is 12.6×. Equation 1 therefore picks recomputation
for nearly every tensor. Figure 1b shows how much swapping
can still contribute: during one iteration, the link can move 7–
37% of the saved bytes on platform A and 6–12% on platform
B. Compressing the transfers with ZVC raises these shares by
1.2× on average, and the PlaneZip codec by 1.4× (A) and
1.7× (B).
III. PLANEZIP DESIGN
Figure 2 shows the three parts of PlaneZip. A compression
engine on the GPU’s copy path compresses every swapped
tensor on its way to host memory and decompresses it on the
way back. A runtime predicts the compressed size of each
saved tensor from the sizes that the engine reported in earlier
iterations. A planner uses these sizes to decide, for every saved
tensor, whether to keep, swap, or drop it, and when to prefetch
it.

larity of BPC [11], and interprets each block as N symbols
of w bits, where w is the element width of the tensor: 64

Each block is encoded in four ways:
• ZVC [13]: an N-bit mask of non-zero symbols followed
by the non-zero symbols.
• DBX: BPC’s transform. A base symbol, then the w+1
bit-planes of the N−1 deltas between neighboring sym-
bols, with adjacent planes XORed.
• BX: the w bit-planes of the symbols themselves, with ad-
jacent planes XORed and no delta. Neighboring floating-
point values often share their high-order exponent bits
but not their low-order bits, so their deltas can be
large while the high bit-planes stay constant. Compresso
likewise found that skipping BPC’s transform sometimes
compresses better [12].
• ZBX: an N-bit zero mask, then BX over the n non-
zero symbols compacted to the front of the block, so
each plane is n bits wide. In a ReLU output or a
causally masked attention matrix, zeros are interleaved
with positive values of similar magnitude. ZVC stores
the survivors verbatim, and in the planes of BX or DBX
the zeros break every run; ZBX removes the zeros first
and then compresses the shared high-order bits of the
survivors.
DBX, BX, and ZBX code each plane with BPC’s prefix
code [11]: 3 bits for an all-zero plane and 6 or 7 for a run of
them, 5 bits for an all-ones plane or one whose pre-XOR plane
is zero, 5 bits plus the position for one or two adjacent ones,
and a 1-bit flag before any other plane, stored verbatim. The
field widths scale with the number and width of the planes.
The engine keeps the smallest of the four encodings and the
raw block and spends a 3-bit tag on the choice.
Stream format. Thirty-two consecutive blocks form a 4 KB
chunk. A chunk is byte-aligned and preceded by a 16-bit
compressed size, so the decompressor finds every chunk
with a prefix sum over the headers and decodes chunks in
parallel; a chunk that would grow is stored raw. On the host,
compressed tensors occupy fixed-size chunks of a pinned pool,
so their variable sizes do not fragment the allocator, the same
reason Compresso allocates compressed pages in fixed 512 B
chunks [12]. Every ratio we report includes tags, headers, and
padding.
Validation. We implemented every encoder twice: a vectorized
size model, used for all measurements, and a bit-exact encoder
and decoder. On 4,000 blocks sampled from real FP32 and
BF16 activations of the five models and 1,800 synthetic corner
cases, the decoder reproduces every block and the bit counts
of the two implementations agree exactly.
B. Compressibility Predictor
The planner needs the compressed size of a tensor before
the tensor exists. Training repeats the same operator sequence
every iteration, so PlaneZip identifies a saved tensor by the
position of its producing operator in that sequence and its
output slot, the tensor-level analogue of the PC signature of
a reuse predictor [19]. The runtime stores one compression
ratio per signature, a table of a few hundred entries. The
engine reports the compressed size of every swap, the sum
of its chunk headers, and the runtime overwrites the entry.
Tensors that the plan does not swap are measured in a
profiling iteration, in which the engine compresses them into
a scratch buffer and discards the output. Until a signature has
a measurement, the planner assumes a ratio of one.

D. Hardware Cost
BPC’s compressor occupies 48,000 µm2 in a 40 nm process
at 800 MHz and encodes 128 bytes every four cycles with
a latency of 11 cycles [11]. A PlaneZip lane adds the BX
and ZBX encoders, which share BPC’s bit-plane transposition
and pattern logic, a 64-entry compaction network for ZBX,
and the size comparison. We estimate a lane at three times
the area of BPC’s compressor and assume decompressors of
equal size. Two compressor lanes for device-to-host copies
and two decompressor lanes for host-to-device copies, each at
1.5 GHz, deliver 96 GB/s per direction, above the 64 GB/s that
we assume in the evaluation. Scaling 40 nm logic to a 7 nm
process by a conservative 16× in density gives 0.036 mm2 for
the four lanes, 0.004% of an 826 mm2 A100 die [20]; BPC’s
7 mW of active power at 40 nm [12] suggests well under 0.1 W.
The added latency, tens of cycles per block, is negligible next
to the microseconds of a PCIe transfer.

Tensor capture. We intercept every ATen operator of a
training iteration in PyTorch [31] with a dispatch-mode hook
and, like object-level GPU memory profilers [32], record for
every tensor storage its producing operator, its size, and every
read and write. A tensor produced in the forward pass and
read in the backward pass is a saved activation, exactly the
set that rematerialization can evict; PyTorch’s saved-tensor
hooks confirm the set. We capture tensor contents on the
CPU at a small batch (8 images, or one 1,024-token sequence)
over three consecutive iterations with optimizer updates. We
record operator traces at the training batch sizes on PyTorch’s
meta device, which propagates shapes without data. Each
captured tensor maps to its trace counterpart by producing
operator and output slot; GPT-2’s operator sequence differs
slightly between the two batch sizes, so we align the two
sequences and treat the 4% of its saved bytes left unmatched
as incompressible. Two adjustments make the CPU run match
GPU training: dropout runs through the fused kernel that GPUs
use, which saves a 1-byte mask, and Transformer attention
uses the frameworks’ non-fused implementation, which saves
the softmax output (Section V-F discusses fused attention).
FP32 tensors are compressed as captured. BF16 tensors are
their round-to-nearest-even conversions, which approximates
BF16 mixed-precision training [33]. Tensors above 32 MB are
compressed on every fourth 4 KB chunk.

the platforms of Table II. An operator takes a 4 µs launch
overhead plus the larger of its FLOPs over the achievable
math throughput and its bytes over the achievable memory

device-to-host and a host-to-device copy queue (FIFO, with

V. EVALUATION
A. Compression Ratio
Figure 3 compares six codecs on all saved activations of one
iteration, weighted by bytes. In BF16, PlaneZip compresses
activations by 1.71× (geometric mean), from 1.31× for ViT-
B/16 to 2.14× for DenseNet-121. ZVC compresses only the
models with ReLU activations or causal masks and leaves
EfficientNet-B0 and ViT-B/16 at 1.00×, for a geometric mean
of 1.18×. BPC, which applies its delta transform to every
block, reaches 1.50×, and no single encoding exceeds 1.57×:
choosing per block adds 9% over the best of them. FP32
activations compress less, 1.39× with PlaneZip, because their
23-bit mantissas are nearly random and dilute the redundancy
of the sign and exponent bits.

0.2–0.8% of the wire bytes. A signature that keeps only the
operator type, which would let one entry serve every layer,
errs by 2.6–12.5%, and a single global ratio by up to 43%,
because tensors of different layers and operators compress
differently. Training progress matters more than iterations: at
random initialization, the ratios differ from those of the trained
models by −6% (ResNet-50) to +12% (GPT-2), so the runtime
refreshes its table during training instead of reusing ratios
across runs.

C. Throughput Under a Memory Budget
Figure 4 shows the throughput of each policy as the budget
shrinks. At 50% of the peak, the mean overhead on platform
A is 14.1% for RECOMP, 13.3% for DTE, 6.7% for BW,
4.1% for BW+ZVC, and 3.5% for PlaneZip; on platform B
it is 19.6%, 19.6%, 13.0%, 10.5%, and 9.0%. SWAP alone
slows training by 2.8× (A) and 5.1× (B), because the link
cannot move half of the activations within an iteration. DTE
matches RECOMP in 58 of the 70 configurations: its cost
model charges every swap its full transfer time and there-
fore picks recomputation. Bandwidth-aware swapping halves
DTE’s overhead on platform A and cuts it by a third on
platform B, and PlaneZip then removes another 47% (A) and
31% (B) of what remains. At 30% of the peak, PlaneZip cuts
BW’s overhead from 17.4% to 12.2% (A) and from 33.2% to
23.7% (B). Viewed the other way, PlaneZip stays within 5%
of full speed down to a budget of 43% of the peak on platform
A and 59% on platform B on average, against 54% and 67%
for BW and 64% and 75% for DTE.
The gain grows with compressibility and is largest when
the link can cover part, but not all, of the memory deficit.
GPT-2 gains the most: its attention tensors, half of its saved
bytes, compress by 2.8×, so at 30% on platform B its overhead
falls from 46% with BW to 28%. DenseNet-121 gains 11
points at 50% on platform B (27% to 17%), but BW+ZVC
captures nearly all of it: the tensors worth swapping are sparse
ReLU outputs that ZVC already compresses by 3.1×, beyond
the 2.7× at which the engine instead of the link limits the
transfer. EfficientNet-B0 gains the least, 1.5 points at 50%
on platform B: its activations compress by only 1.40×, and
even compressed the link can move just 8% of them. On
platform A, ViT-B/16 computes slowly enough that BW hides
most transfers (1.0% overhead at 50%), which leaves little for
compression.
D. Where the Gains Come From
Figure 5 isolates the components at a 30% budget. Com-
pressing the swaps of a compression-unaware plan recovers
only 0.3 of the 5.2 points that separate BW from PlaneZip on
platform A and 0.2 of 9.5 points on platform B: shorter trans-
fers free link time, but the plan does not fill it. In 67 of the 70
configurations, the planner keeps a compression-aware plan.
Adding compression to DTE’s cost model lowers its overhead
from 24.4% to 21.3% (A) and from 41.7% to 30.6% (B), more
than half of it through GPT-2, whose compressed attention
probabilities become cheaper to swap than to recompute, but
it still trails PlaneZip by 9.1 and 6.9 points. With ZVC instead
of the PlaneZip codec, the planner keeps 52% of the gain
on platform A and 33% on platform B, where dense BF16
tensors that ZVC cannot compress dominate. Coding with 32-
bit symbols costs 0.5 points on platform B; on platform A, the
FP32 symbols are 32 bits anyway. The predictor matters little
as long as it is per tensor: oracle ratios leave the overhead
unchanged, while the operator-type signature raises it by 0.5
(A) and 1.1 (B) points. Compared with BW, PlaneZip swaps
1.5× (A) and 2.1× (B) more bytes and spends 30% (A) and
31% (B) less time on recomputation, while stalls stay at or
below 1.1% of the iteration.
E. Sensitivity
Figure 6a varies the link bandwidth of platform B from 12 to
192 GB/s per direction. The gain peaks at intermediate band-
widths. At 12 GB/s, which also approximates two PCIe 4.0
GPUs sharing a switch uplink, PlaneZip lowers the mean
overhead from 16.1% (BW) to 12.0%; at 24 and 48 GB/s it
removes 5.7 and 6.4 points; at 192 GB/s the uncompressed link
already carries everything worth swapping, and both policies
stay at or below 0.4%. Figure 6b varies the engine throughput.
An engine that is only as fast as the link never helps, because
compressed transfers become engine-bound and the runtime
bypasses it. At the default 64 GB/s the overhead is 9.0%, and
the 96 GB/s of the two lanes estimated in Section III-D lowers
it to 7.3%.
F. Discussion and Limitations
Fused attention. FlashAttention [36] recomputes the attention
matrix instead of saving it. In GPT-2, the attention proba-
bilities, their dropout outputs, and their masks account for
51% of the saved bytes and compress by 2.82×, helped by
the zeros of the causal mask; the remaining tensors compress
by 1.46×, like the dense tensors of the other models. With
fused attention, GPT-2 would gain less, closer to ViT-B/16 in
Figure 4.
Fidelity. Our timing model ignores kernel fusion and inter-
ference between copies and kernels, our ratios come from
CPU-executed iterations at small batch sizes, and our BF16
tensors are conversions. Capturing tensors in GPU runs with an
accelerator analysis framework such as PASTA [37] would re-
move the last two approximations. The timing model matches
published ResNet-50 throughput within 3%, the ratios reflect
real data, weights, and training updates, and all policies share
the same model, but absolute overheads on real hardware will
differ. The planner also assumes that the operator sequence
repeats; models with dynamic control flow need one plan per
sequence, or MegTaiChi’s online eviction with the compressed
cost of Equation 1.

VI. RELATED WORK
Rematerialization. Checkpointing [1] and its optimal ILP
formulation [8] trade memory for recomputation statically;
DTR [9] does so online with a greedy heuristic. vDNN [5]
and SwapAdvisor [6] swap activations to host memory; Ca-
puchin [7] and MegTaiChi [10] combine swapping and re-
computation, and MegTaiChi adds tensor partitioning and a
fragmentation-free allocator. These planners treat a tensor’s
transfer size as its raw size; PlaneZip supplies compressed
sizes and a compression engine, and its planner could be
replaced by any of them that accepts per-tensor transfer costs.
Profilers such as DrGPUM [32] remove memory that an
application wastes; rematerialization handles the memory it
needs.
Activation compression. cDMA [13] is the closest work: a
ZVC compressor in the DMA engine that accelerates vDNN’s
swaps. PlaneZip extends the codec beyond zeros, which mat-
ters for activations without ReLU sparsity (Table III), and
makes the planner aware of the compression, without which
the gain largely disappears (Section V-D). Gist [39] encodes
ReLU outputs as bitmaps or sparse matrices and stores other
activations in reduced precision, and ActNN [40] quantizes
activations to 2 bits. Lossy schemes reach higher ratios but
change the numerics of training; PlaneZip is lossless.
Hardware memory compression. BDI [15] and BPC [11]
compress cache lines and memory blocks; Compresso [12]
manages compressed main memory, and Buddy Compres-
sion [41] applies BPC to GPU memory and spills incompress-
ible lines to a slower peer memory. PlaneZip reuses BPC’s
transform and code map and Compresso’s chunked allocation,
but compresses only the data that crosses the slow link, where
the latency of compression is hidden. ZipNN [42] exploits
the same exponent redundancy in stored model weights.
Baryon [17] combines compression and sub-blocking in hybrid
memory, and PlaneZip carries the predictor-splitting idea of
Yokoyama et al. [18] over to compressibility.
VII. CONCLUSION
GPU compute has outgrown the host link, so rematerial-
ization planners recompute almost everything and swap only
what fits in idle link time. PlaneZip enlarges that budget


by compressing swapped activations with a per-block choice
among four bit-plane and zero-value encodings, and turns the
compression into speed by planning swaps in compressed
bytes. Activations from five models compress by 1.71× in
BF16 and 1.39× in FP32, each tensor’s compressibility carries
over from one iteration to the next, and at half the activation
memory the rematerialization overhead falls by 47% and 31%
on the two platforms relative to a bandwidth-aware planner
without compression. Next steps are a prototype on a GPU
copy engine or in software, and compressed in-place storage
for tensors that are expensive to recompute.

# A mechanism for TCP-aware Active Queue Management in packet-forwarding devices 

Swarnarup Bhunia ∗, Somit Gond ∗, Vayun Goel, Sreelakshmi Manjunath † and Padmanabhan Rajan 

School of Computing and Electrical Engineering , Indian Institute of Technology Mandi, HP, India 

{swarnarupbhunia2003, somitgond2 }@gmail.com, {sreelakshmi, padman }@iitmandi.ac.in 

Abstract —The deployment of Active Queue Management (AQM) policies is known to be a promising solution to bufferbloat, the problem of persistently-full large buffers in Internet routers. However, most AQMs involve tunable parameters, and some require awareness of the characteristics of the transport-layer protocol implemented at the end systems, thereby rendering their deployment infeasible. We consider one such AQM—a threshold-based queue policy which requires the packet-dropping threshold to be co-designed with relevant TCP parameters. We propose a mechanism that detects network congestion, estimates the congestion window evolution in the end hosts, estimates the required TCP parameter, and produces an optimal value of the packet-dropping threshold. Arguments based on analysis of the TCP fluid model are presented to substantiate the proposed mechanism. A simulation-based performance evaluation reveals the effectiveness of the proposed mechanism, compared to some widely implemented queue policies, in terms of various user- and system-oriented metrics. The proposed mechanism is particularly well-suited for implementation in Software Defined Networks (SDN) where the algorithms can run in the SDN controller and the packet-dropping threshold can be implemented in the data-plane switches through open interfaces. 

Index Terms —Bufferbloat, Active Queue Management, Latency, Congestion Control 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Oversized buffers in packet-forwarding devices, such as routers and switches, are known to cause large queues that lead to increased latency across the Internet [1]. This phenomenon, termed bufferbloat [2], is attributed to the use of the simple DropTail policy in these queues and is known to be the cause of congestion collapse in the nascent stages of the Internet [3], [4]. The use of effective Active Queue Management (AQM) strategies has been identified as a solution [2], [5], [6]. Although numerous AQM policies have been proposed; see the survey in [7], most of them require precise parameter tuning, making them difficult to deploy in edge or core routers. Consequently, AQMs are not actively deployed in today’s networks. This prompted the Internet Engineering Task Force to update the recommendations for the selection and deployment of AQMs, see RFC 7567 [8]. The most important, arguably, of these recommendations was that an AQM must be able to self-tune its parameters on the basis of operational conditions.

In the recent past, machine learning (ML) approaches have been adopted for AQM design, leading to AQMs which can self-tune their parameters. For a detailed survey, see [9]. Most of these AQMs, for example, LearnQueue [10], RLECN [11], PET [12] etc., use reinforcement learning to tune buffer size or packet-marking thresholds. Implementation of such AQMs requires large amounts of training data and their operation incurs significant computational overhead [9]. Moreover, the self correcting mechanisms inherent to reinforcement learning may cause instabilities in the network leading to increased jitter and unfairness. Consequently, one cannot definitively conclude that these ML-based AQMs can be deployed in real-world networks or that they outperform traditional ones [9], [13]. Parallelly, the emergence of Software Defined Network (SDN) architectures has enabled the implementation of the control plane of the network layer in remote servers. With this, queue-management decisions at packet-forwarding devices can now rely on algorithms implemented in remote servers, thereby leveraging their computational capabilities. In particular, the SDN controller can implement algorithms that enable self-tuning of AQM policies deployed in SDN switches. However, despite this capability, existing research on AQM for SDNs has largely focused on developing enhancements of classical AQM schemes that are amenable to implementation in programmable SDN switches. For example, P4-CoDel [14] is based on CoDel, iRED [15] on Random Early Detection (RED), and PI2 [16] is an enhancement of the Proportional Integral (PI) queue policy. Although implementable, these enhanced AQMs carry the demerits of the underlying core techniques. For instance, iRED uses an exponential weighted moving average (EWMA) of the queue size to make packet-dropping decisions [15], similar to the original RED policy, which is known to make networks prone to instability and their operation highly sensitive to the choice of EWMA parameters and packet-dropping thresholds [17]. Consequently, practical deployment of AQMs remains challenging. Studies reveal that to effectively deploy most AQMs, their parameters and packet-dropping/marking thresholds need to be tuned in accordance with TCP parameters, inherent to the congestion control algorithm in the transport layer of the end hosts, which are unknown to the router/switch deploying the AQM [18]–[21]. Leveraging this, we develop a mechanism that can learn the congestion-control behaviour of the transport protocol implemented in the end hosts and make optimal packet-dropping decisions at the routers/switches, thereby enabling arXiv:2610.24621v1 [cs.NI] 17 Oct 2026 2

an AQM to self-tune its parameters for prevalent operational conditions. In this paper, we focus on a recently proposed threshold-based queue policy [19], [20], which requires that its packet-dropping threshold be co-designed with relevant TCP parameters. We outline a mechanism that estimates the congestion window maintained by the end hosts and estimates the required TCP parameter(s) from the estimated congestion window, and then computes the packet-dropping threshold based on the estimate. The so-computed threshold is used to activate the AQM once the router/switch experiences congestion. The proposed mechanism uses (i) a simple supervised learning technique to estimate the congestion window, and (ii) fundamen-tal principles of TCP congestion control to estimate the relevant parameters. The use of supervised learning lowers the training requirement, compared to other reinforcement-learning-based AQMs which typically require rigorous training and precise tuning. Furthermore, our approach is grounded in mathematical understanding of the underlying TCP-AQM feedback system, which confirms that maintaining control-theoretic stability of the feedback system guarantees improved network performance. Therefore, the proposed algorithms promise stable operation of the network at an operating point that ensures improved performance. The performance of the proposed mechanism is evaluated using a simulation-based study conducted on ns-3 [22]. It is compared with the widely used DropTail policy and the Adaptive RED (ARED) policy which is currently implemented—although not deployed—in most IP routers and SDN switches. The mechanism, supported by analytical arguments and inferences from the simulations, leads to TCP-aware queue management for low-latency operation of packet-forwarding devices. It is particularly well suited to SDN architectures where all the computations required for the learning-based decision making are performed in the SDN control server and the decisions are installed in the switches through P4 programming [23]. This makes the algorithms implementable in current light-weight SDN switches. The key contributions of this paper are as follows. (i) An algorithm for congestion detection through observa-tions of queue size at a packet-forwarding device. (ii) Application of a simple supervised-learning technique to estimate the congestion window maintained at the end hosts from sequence numbers from the IP datagram payload. (iii) Methodology for estimating a representative TCP parame-ter from the estimated congestion window traces supported by relevant mathematical derivations. (iv) An algorithm for computing the packet-dropping threshold, using the estimated TCP parameter in conjunction with an established control-theoretic result, for stable operation of the network with improved network performance. (v) Simulation-based performance evaluation with relevant topologies, traffic compositions and metrics, and compari-son with relevant baseline policies. The rest of the paper is organised as follows. The preliminaries and the problem formulation are presented in Section II. Section III outlines the proposed mechanism, and Section IV presents the simulations and results. We conclude in Section V. A. Problem formulation 

Deploying the threshold-based queue policy in a packet-forwarding device boils down to implementing algorithms for the following. (i) Detect congestion at the router/switch by observing the queue size. (ii) Estimate the congestion window evolution for all end hosts sharing the bottleneck link. (iii) Estimate the multiplicative decrease parameter β that governs the decrease in the average congestion window after packet loss in the event of congestion. (iv) Compute an appropriate value of the packet-dropping threshold qth which decongests the router/switch and maintains low queueing delays. For developing the underlying algorithms for the mechanism, we assume that all the end systems use AIMD-based congestion control and all the flows are long-lived, i.e. , flows pertaining to FTP applications that last long enough to drive the system into a steady state. This simplistic choice of traffic composition aligns with the assumptions of the fluid models employed in the study of TCP-AQM feedback systems and ensures the applicability of the analytical result (3). Once we develop the required algorithms, we systematically deviate from the assumptions and evaluate their performance in more evolved network topologies and realistic traffic mixes through simulations. A. Congestion detection 

It is well known that congestion causes synchronisation of TCP flows across hosts sharing a link, and results in pronounced deterministic oscillations in the queue size [26]– [28]. Building on this premise, we use queue size oscillations as an indicator of incipient congestion. Such oscillations can be detected and distinguished from other variations in the queue size by computing the autocorrelation of the queue size data. Oscillations in the queue size with relatively higher amplitude and lower frequency, which indicate the onset of congestion, cause the autocorrelation of the queue size to have lesser number of zero crossings. This relationship is illustrated in Fig. 2, where we plotted the queue size at the router observed through packet-level simulations of a single-bottleneck link of 100 Mbps capacity serving N = 60 TCP hosts. The conventional DropTail policy is used for queue management. We used the TCP NewReno variant and simulated two regimes, one where the TCP flows have a small average RTT of 10 ms, and another where the average RTT is 200 ms. In Algorithm 1, mz is the zero-crossing threshold used to determine the presence of congestion. The value of mz

is decided by the operating point of the algorithm; i.e. the relative cost of missing the presence of congestion, versus the cost of falsely declaring congestion when there is actually none. Assuming that the cost of false alarms are negligible, the operating point should be selected to minimize misses. When the number of zero crossings in a time duration of T

is less than mz , then the algorithm concludes that the link is congested. The parameter T represents a time duration for checking the queue size traces for deterministic oscillations. Although the choice of T is left to the implementation and may vary across routers, it is important to note that T must be at least a few multiples of RTT. This is because it takes at least a few RTTs for TCP hosts to infer congestion in the network and react to packet loss by reducing their congestion window. Setting T to a large value, say a few seconds, would ensure that the router detects congestion successfully. However, this may result in a delay in detecting congestion, which may impact network performance. Towards understanding the influence of the parameter T on the ability of Algorithm 1 to accurately detect congestion, we examined the Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve of the detector. For this we conducted 100 simulations, of the aforementioned single-bottleneck link, of which 50 were of a congested scenario while the remaining did not have congestion. Taking it as ground truth we compared our congestion detection approach with a range of thresholds mz , for various values of the time duration T . Using this, we plotted the ROC curve to visualize the results. The results are presented in Fig. 3. It 5

can be seen that most values of T give similar true positive rates (close to 100%.) To reduce latency, we thus choose to implement Algorithm 1 with T = 5 seconds. 

B. Congestion window estimation 

Whilst detecting congestion, it is also crucial to maintain an accurate estimate of the congestion window of each TCP flow at the router which upon detection of congestion, can help calculate the required parameters to eventually estimate the optimal packet-dropping threshold qth to resolve congestion. This can be achieved by (1) sniffing the sequence number and related fields from the IP datagram payload at the router and then (2) utilising an intelligent algorithm which can estimate the corresponding congestion window size maintained at the TCP sender’s end for that corresponding TCP flow. Our congestion window estimation methodology involves sniffing the following header fields: the source and destination IP addresses and port numbers, TCP sequence number, acknowledgement number, and the packet size. The four tuple of the IP addresses and port numbers help uniquely identify the TCP flow and these sniffed header fields along with the time of packet arrival can be cached and stored as a time series data per TCP flow. The congestion window size estimation is made using a random forest regressor. The time series data is preprocessed to create the following input features namely direction (whether from source to destination or vice versa), sequence number difference, acknowledgment number difference, time difference (current value minus the previous value), packet size and duplicate ACK count. A window size of 10 packets is considered, i.e. input features of the 9 previous packets along with the currently received input packet features are concatenated and fed into the model as input which in turn estimates the congestion window size at that time instant. These estimates are cached for future estimations in the auto-regressive manner as explained. The implementation from scikit-learn’s 

RandomForestRegressor model initialized with 100 estimators is used. Training data is generated using ns-3 for each simulation topology considered in the study, the details of which are deferred to Section IV. More that 0.8 million training examples were used to train the model.

Given that qth is always a positive integer and that it is always less than the buffer size of the router B; the required value of qth can be obtained by brute force in linear time. We begin by computing the function on the left-hand side of inequality (3) using an initial value of qth , taken as 10; and then recompute this function by incrementing qth until inequality (3) is satisfied. These details, along with some checks, are presented in Algorithm 2. The algorithm uses one parameter κ which serves as an upper bound for the ratio of the average congestion window to the share of bandwidth-delay product of each flow. The physical interpretation of the parameter κ emerges from the congestion-control behaviour of AIMD-based TCP itself. Under such congestion control, the congestion window increases additively until a loss is detected, and is reduced by a multiplicative factor thereafter. It is well known that the peak value of the congestion window of a source would ideally be equal to its share of the bandwidth-delay product. However, when packet-forwarding devices use large buffers, often equal to multiples of the bandwidth-delay product as seen in current devices, packets are not dropped adequately; and the congestion window of an end host increases beyond its share of the bandwidth-delay product leading to excessive buffering. This can be checked by the condition w∗ ≥ ˜Cτ . When an AQM is to be deployed in such a scenario, the packet-dropping threshold must account for such excessive buffering and drop packets in a manner that it serves as adequate feedback for the end hosts to reduce their congestion window. Therefore, when excessive buffering has occurred we use the parameter κ < 1 in place of the ratio

D. Benchmarking, variable factors and metrics 

We benchmark the performance of the threshold-based policy with the proposed mechanism against two policies that are implemented in today’s routers/switches. The first one is the simple DropTail policy which simply drops packets once the buffer is full. Secondly, we consider the Adaptive Random Early Detection (ARED) which is implemented in most Linux-based packet-forwarding devices. For the ARED implementation, we used the parameters minQth = 50 pkts, maxQth = 100 pkts which are comparable with the qth computed for the threshold-based policy. Although numerous other AQMs are proposed in the literature, both classical as well as ML-aided, we choose these two for a comparative study given their implementation status. If an AQM is to be deployed in live-networks, it must outperform those that are already implemented and are hence closest to deployment. Along with varying the topology and traffic composition, as described above, we also consider two variable factors: the round-trip time, and the TCP variant. Round-trip time is a crucial factor as it is an uncontrollable network parameter; in that, longer paths with large propagation delay could cause large RTTs regardless of network conditions. Given that operating regimes with large RTTs are known to exhibit congestion in networks carrying TCP flows [27], [28], we vary RTT in the range [150 , 300] ms. In order to avoid RTT-induced synchronization among hosts, in each simulation with a fixed RTT of τ ∈ [150 , 300] ms, the RTT for each sender-receiver pair is chosen randomly as T ∼ U [τ − 5, τ + 5] ms. To fix the required RTT, each access link is configured with a one-way delay of T / 4, which results in a mean RTT of τ across flows. Two AIMD-based TCP variants, namely LinuxReno 

and NewReno are considered. We consider six performance metrics: (i) throughput, (ii) goodput, (iii) link utilisation, (iv) packet-loss percentage, (v) queueing delay, and (vi) flow-completion time. Of these, the first three are higher-is-better metrics, and the remaining are lower-is-better. The choice of metrics is consistent with the recommendations in RFC 7928 [30] for evaluation of AQMs. For recording all metrics, each simulation is run until all clients have transmitted 100 MB Data. Among these metrics, throughput and goodput were recorded at the receiving hosts. Here, throughput considers all packet transfers while goodput measures the rate of transfer of useful packets, i.e. ,retransmissions do not contribute to goodput. Note that these two are user-oriented metrics. Link utilisation, queueing delay, and packet loss were recorded at the bottleneck link. These serve as system-oriented metrics. Flow completion time is defined as the time required to complete the transfer of a file 10 

of a fixed size, and is often regarded as the most important user-oriented metric. For this, we fix a file size of 100 MB at the FTP application of each host and run the simulation until the last flow was completed. 

E. Results and discussion 

Firstly, to verify the efficiency of the proposed congestion window estimation using the Random Forest regressor, we simulated the two succeeding components, i.e. β estimation and qth computation, under two settings: (i) using the ac-tual congestion window ( cwnd ) traces obtained using the 

CongestionWindow trace source of the socket at each TCP source, (ii) using the congestion window waveform estimated by the Random Forest. Here, the first setting serves as a baseline to compare the second setting with. The parameter estimate ˆβ

and the computed packet-dropping threshold qth obtained for the two cases are plotted in Fig. 6. From these, we observe that using the congestion window estimates produced by the Random Forest, as prescribed by the proposed mechanism, does not cause any significant deviation in the threshold qth across the range of RTT considered. Notably, the curves get closer as the RTT increases, indicating that the window estimation is particularly effective in the large RTT regime which is prone to performance degradation in general. Next, we evaluate the performance of the proposed method using the required metrics in the RTT range considered. In each simulation, throughput, goodput and packet loss are recorded at the receiving hosts using FlowMonitor . Link utilisation and queueing delay are recorded at the router preceding the bottleneck link, through the appropriate calculations on the readouts of the PointToPointNetDevice/PhyTx and the RootQueueDiscList/ ∗/PacketsInQueue trace source, respectively. From the traces obtained for each simula-tion, the first few seconds of data corresponding to transient behaviour are discarded and mean and standard deviation of each metric are computed from the remaining data. For flow completion time, the time required for the 100 MB transfer is recorded for each flow, and the mean and standard deviation across flows is computed. The results for the single bottleneck dumbbell topology with the LinuxReno TCP variant used in heterogeneous traffic composition, for the whole range of RTT are presented in Fig. 7. For the same setting, the results obtained with the parking-lot topology are presented in Fig. 8. In all these plots, we used the dot-whisker representation, where the dot denotes the mean and the upper and lower whiskers are used to indicate the range of values that are within one standard deviation from the mean. We skip the results obtained for homogeneous traffic, and TCP NewReno as they qualitatively appear similar to the ones presented. We now discuss our observations. 

User-oriented performance : In the single bottleneck network, the mean throughput and goodput offered by our AQM implementation is seen to be marginally below that of DropTail, and better than that of ARED. Notably, the throughput and goodput remain constant across RTT in the case of our AQM, and ARED exhibits a reduction in throughput and goodput as RTT increases. In particular, the goodput performance of our AQM is agnostic to the RTT, even in operating regimes with large RTTs. This is due to the fact that the qth is tuned according to condition (3) which guarantees network stability regardless of the RTT. In contrast, the goodput achieved by ARED suffers as RTT increases. Similar observations hold for the case of flow completion time, which is a lower-is-better metric as opposed to the former two. Our AQM has a marginally higher mean flow completion time as compared to DropTail, with the standard deviation of the two indicating that the observed flow completion time for a give flow is likely to be similar for the two policies. Meanwhile, the flow completion time of ARED is larger than that of our AQM, and shows a substantial increase as RTT increases. For the parking-lot topology, the three policies show similar relative performance. Additionally, for this topology, the standard deviation of throughput and goodput of ARED is much larger than that of our AQM and DropTail. This indicates that the hosts would experience pronounced throughput fluctuations during the life of a connection. Likewise, a large standard deviation in flow completion time indicates a large variation in the time taken by hosts to complete the file transfer, and hence is reflective of unfair network behaviour. 

System-oriented performance : From the plots showing the link utilisation, it can be observed that DropTail guarantees a marginally better utilisation of the link than our AQM, with the gap being less for the parking-lot topology. ARED lacks in this aspect, with the utilisation reducing as RTT increases. Though this is seen in both topologies, it is more pronounced in the parking-lot topology. This is because DropTail does not take any action towards congestion mitigation, it simply drops packets once the buffer is full. In doing so, it keeps the buffer full most of the time, thereby never starving the link, and hence ensuring a higher utilisation. On the other hand, our AQM and ARED are meant to drop packets in a manner that congestion is alleviated. By dropping packets appropriately, they ensure that the queue size remains small, which could sometimes lead to loss of link utilisation. This is indeed reflected in the queueing delay and packet loss measurements. One can observe that our AQM and ARED ensure a much smaller queueing delay compared to DropTail. Although our AQM has a higher queueing delay than ARED, it can be observed that this delay does not reflect in the flow completion time. The observations regarding queueing delay are consistent across the two topologies. From the packet loss percentage, it is seen that in the single bottleneck dumbbell topology, DropTail drops the least number of packets while ARED drops the most, our AQM lies between the two. For large RTTs, the fraction of packets dropped by our AQM is very similar to that of DropTail, which indicates that our AQM is able to maintain substantially less queueing delay while dropping just marginally more packets. In the parking-lot topology, we see that our AQM exhibits a lower packet loss compared to DropTail, with the reduction being more evident for large RTTs. ARED shows a larger packet loss for small RTTs, and nearly converges to that of our AQM as RTT increases. Arguably, our AQM ensures better packet loss than DropTail in networks where links appear in tandem. V. C ONCLUSIONS 

Most Active Queue Management (AQM) strategies aimed at alleviating network congestion have tunable parameters that need to be co-designed with parameters of the transport protocols, such as TCP, implemented in the end hosts. Toward facilitating the deployment of such AQMs, we examined the possibility of estimating TCP parameters through packet traces accessible to the router and co-designing AQM parameters with these. In particular, we considered a threshold-based queue policy which requires the packet-dropping threshold to be co-designed with TCP parameters. This work outlines a mechanism that detects network congestion, predicts the evolution of the congestion window at end hosts, estimates the required TCP parameter, and produces a value of the packet-dropping threshold. Appropriate algorithms are outlined for congestion detection and threshold computation. The mechanism used to estimate the TCP parameter is supported by arguments based on existing results from the analysis of TCP fluid models. We conducted packet-level simulations to evaluate the performance of the threshold-based policy deployed as per the proposed mechanism. The results show that our implementation of the AQM performs equivalent to DropTail in terms of some metrics, and particularly outperforms it in terms of the queueing delay. It is also seen to be better than ARED, which is implemented in most Linux-based routers today, in terms of most performance metrics. Although the proposed mechanism is grounded in mathematical models that account for AIMD behaviour of TCP alone, our simulations reveal that it performs well in networks carrying unresponsive UDP flows and bursty short-lived TCP flows as well. The proposed mechanism appears to be a promising solution towards TCP-aware queue management through appropriate deployment of AQMs to alleviate congestion in packet-forwarding devices. It is particularly suited for SDN architectures, which provide the flexibility of control- and data-plane separation. 

A. Future work 

This work relies on results from analysis of a TCP fluid model that captures the behaviour of the AIMD congestion control algorithm. However, the proposed mechanism only needs to estimate the multiplicative decrease parameter, regard-less of those that govern the additive increase. This raises the question of whether the proposed mechanism would work for traffic comprising TCP flows that employ a congestion control algorithm that decreases the congestion window multiplicatively upon detecting congestion, but increases it using a different approach. For example, CUBIC (default in Linux OS), increases its congestion window in a non-linear manner, but reduces it 12 

multiplicatively in the event of congestion. It is desirable to explore whether the proposed mechanism would work for such TCP flows. Further, it is desirable to evaluate the performance of the proposed mechanism in switches that support Explicit Congestion Notification (ECN), this would also pave the way for examining its performance with TCP variants that rely on ECN marks, instead of packet drops, for congestion feedback.
